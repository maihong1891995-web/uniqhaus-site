Opening up a closed floor plan is one of the most requested remodels in the Bay Area, and the first question is always the same: what does it cost to take out a wall? The honest answer is that it depends entirely on one thing - whether the wall carries weight. A simple partition is cheap to remove. A load-bearing wall is a structural project that needs an engineer, a permit, and a new beam to carry the load the wall was holding up. This guide breaks down real numbers for both, explains how to tell them apart, and shows how it all fits into an open-concept remodel.

How much does it cost to remove a wall?

The cost splits into two very different worlds. A non-load-bearing wall runs about $500 to $2,000 to remove, while a load-bearing wall runs $4,000 to $20,000 or more once you add the engineering, beam, posts, and permit that make it safe. The table below shows what drives each range.

Wall typeTypical costWhat is included
Non-load-bearing wall$500 - $2,000Demolition, debris hauling, and patching the floor, ceiling, and adjacent walls
Load-bearing wall with beam$4,000 - $10,000Structural engineer, new beam (LVL) and posts, building permit and plan check
Load-bearing with foundation/steel or utilities in wall$10,000 - $20,000+New footings, steel beam, and rerouting electrical, plumbing, or ductwork

Two things push a project toward the high end: the size of the load and what is hidden inside the wall. A long span or a two-story load above may call for a steel beam and new footings under the posts, which means concrete work. And any wall with wiring, plumbing, or HVAC ducts running through it adds rerouting labor before demolition can even start. These are the same variables we scope on a whole house remodel, where wall removals are almost always part of the plan.

How to tell if a wall is load-bearing

You cannot price a wall removal until you know whether the wall carries load, and there are a few reliable clues - but they are only clues. A wall is more likely to be load-bearing if it:

  • Runs perpendicular to the floor or ceiling joists above it. Load-bearing walls carry the joists that cross them; a wall running parallel to joists is often just a partition.
  • Sits on or near an exterior wall line. Walls that continue the line of an outside wall, or stack directly above a wall on the floor below, usually carry weight down to the foundation.
  • Stacks above a beam or girder. If you can see a beam, girder, or post line in the crawl space or basement directly under the wall, that wall is almost certainly structural.

Never guess, and never rely on these signs alone. Homes get remodeled, framing gets modified, and point loads hide in unexpected places. Only a licensed structural engineer can confirm whether a wall is load-bearing and, just as important, specify the size of the beam and posts needed to replace it. Removing a bearing wall without that analysis is how ceilings sag and floors crack. This is why our architecture and permitting work always starts with a structural assessment before any wall is touched.

What the cost includes

When you see a $4,000 to $20,000 range for a load-bearing wall, that number is not just demolition - it is a chain of coordinated trades. A complete wall-removal scope includes:

  • Structural engineering. Calculations and a stamped detail specifying the new beam and posts, typically $500 to $2,500 depending on complexity.
  • Permit and plan check with the City of San Jose. Structural changes require a building permit, and the stamped plans go through plan review before work begins.
  • Beam and posts. A new LVL (engineered wood) or steel beam to carry the load, plus posts that transfer that load down to a footing.
  • Demolition and patching. Removing the wall, then closing the floor, ceiling, and adjacent walls so the old wall line disappears.
  • Rerouting electrical, plumbing, and HVAC. Anything living inside the wall - outlets, switches, pipes, ducts - has to be relocated to another route.
  • Finishing across the old wall line. Blending flooring and ceiling so the finished room reads as one continuous space, not a patched-over seam.

The finishing step is the one homeowners underestimate. Flooring rarely matches perfectly across an old wall line, and the ceiling has to be repaired where the wall met it, so a clean open-concept result usually means new flooring or ceiling work across the affected area, not just a patch.

Is an open floor plan worth it?

For most Bay Area homeowners, the answer is yes - but it is worth understanding the trade-offs before committing $10,000 or more to a structural change. The benefits are real:

  • More natural light. Removing an interior wall lets light travel across the whole footprint instead of stopping at a partition.
  • Better flow. Connecting the kitchen to the living and dining areas is the single biggest driver of open-plan requests, and it makes small homes feel far larger.
  • Resale appeal. Open kitchens and great rooms remain what most Bay Area buyers look for, which supports resale value.

The trade-offs to plan for: a large open space is harder to heat and cool evenly and can need HVAC adjustments; sound carries further without walls to break it up; and the cost of a bearing-wall removal is significant on its own. The most efficient time to open a floor plan is as part of a larger project - a whole house remodel or kitchen renovation - when the walls are already open, the crew is already mobilized, and the beam work overlaps with the systems work you are doing anyway.

The process and permits

A wall-removal project moves through four stages: design, structural engineering, permit, and build. First the design settles what the open space should be and where the new beam and posts can land. Then a structural engineer runs the calculations and produces the stamped detail. Those plans go to the City of San Jose for a building permit and plan check at SJPermits.org - see our San Jose permit process guide for the timeline. Only after the permit is issued does demolition begin, with the beam installed and temporary shoring in place to carry the load safely during the swap.

The reason surprises happen on wall removals is handoffs - a designer who does not talk to the engineer, an engineer whose beam does not fit the ceiling, a contractor who discovers plumbing in the wall on demo day. A design-build team that handles architecture, engineering coordination, permitting, and construction under one roof removes those gaps. Frequently Asked Questions

How much does it cost to remove a load-bearing wall?

Removing a load-bearing wall in the Bay Area typically costs $4,000 to $20,000 or more. The price climbs because you need a structural engineer, a new beam and posts to carry the load, a permit and plan check, and sometimes new footings or steel. Rerouting wiring, plumbing, or HVAC ducts hidden in the wall adds more on top.

How much to remove a non-load-bearing wall?

Removing a non-load-bearing wall usually costs about $500 to $2,000. That covers demolition, hauling, and patching the floor, ceiling, and adjacent walls back to a finished surface. Cost rises if the wall hides electrical, plumbing, or ductwork that has to be rerouted before the wall comes out.

How do I know if a wall is load-bearing?

Signs a wall is load-bearing include running perpendicular to the floor or ceiling joists above, sitting on or near an exterior wall line, or stacking above a beam or girder in the crawl space or basement. These are only clues. Never guess - only a licensed structural engineer can confirm whether a wall carries load and specify the beam needed to replace it.

Do I need a permit to remove a wall?

Yes. Removing a load-bearing wall requires a building permit and plan check with the City of San Jose because it changes the structure, and the plans usually need a structural engineer's stamp. Even a non-load-bearing wall can trigger a permit if it hides electrical or plumbing that must be rerouted. Skipping the permit risks red-tags and problems at resale.

Is an open floor plan worth it?

For most homeowners, yes. An open floor plan brings more natural light, better flow between the kitchen and living areas, and often a resale boost. The trade-offs are cost, plus more work to keep heating, cooling, and sound comfortable in one large space. It makes the most sense as part of a broader remodel where the wall change is planned with the rest of the work.

Key Takeaways

  • A non-load-bearing wall costs about $500 to $2,000 to remove; a load-bearing wall runs $4,000 to $20,000 or more.
  • The load-bearing price includes a structural engineer, a new beam and posts, a permit, and often steel or new footings.
  • Perpendicular joists, an exterior wall line, or a beam below are clues - but only a structural engineer can confirm load-bearing status.
  • Removing a wall almost always needs a City of San Jose permit; the cheapest time to open a floor plan is during a larger remodel.
