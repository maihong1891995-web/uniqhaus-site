Rewiring & Repiping in a Whole House Remodel

A whole house remodel is the right moment to rewire and repipe because the walls, floors, and ceilings are already open. Older Bay Area homes with knob-and-tube or aluminum wiring, galvanized or polybutylene pipe, or an undersized panel should replace those systems now. Budget roughly $14,000 to $45,000 for a rewire, $9,000 to $35,000 for a repipe, and $3,500 to $8,000 for a 200-amp panel upgrade in 2026.

Much of the Bay Area housing stock predates the systems we now take for granted. A San Jose ranch from the 1950s, an Eichler from the 1960s, or a bungalow from before the war often still carries its original wiring and its original supply pipe, and both were designed for a home that used a fraction of the water and power a modern household demands. When you plan a whole house remodel, the wiring and plumbing inside the walls deserve as much attention as the kitchen you can see. This guide explains when rewiring and repiping in a whole house remodel is necessary, what it costs in 2026, the warning signs to look for, and why doing the work while the walls are open is the single biggest cost saving available. It pairs with our gut renovation cost guide and the whole house remodel service.

When a remodel needs rewiring or repiping

A remodel needs rewiring or repiping when the home still relies on outdated systems that are unsafe, undersized, or at the end of their service life. The four most common culprits in Bay Area homes are knob-and-tube wiring, aluminum branch wiring, galvanized steel pipe, and polybutylene pipe. If your home has any of them, replacing the system during the remodel is almost always the right call.

Knob-and-tube wiring was standard through the 1940s. It has no ground conductor, its insulation grows brittle with age, and it is easily overloaded by modern appliances, which is why many insurers now refuse to cover a home that still has it. Aluminum branch wiring, common in the mid 1960s and early 1970s, expands and loosens at connections over time and has a documented fire risk at outlets and switches. On the plumbing side, galvanized steel pipe corrodes from the inside, choking water pressure and shedding rust into your water, while polybutylene pipe, installed into the 1990s, becomes brittle and is prone to sudden failure. An undersized electrical panel, often a 100-amp or smaller fuse box, rounds out the list: it simply cannot carry the load of a remodeled home with a modern kitchen, heat pump, and EV charger. Cost to rewire, repipe, and upgrade the panel in 2026

Costs vary with home size, the number of fixtures and circuits, wall access, and finish level, but the ranges below reflect real 2026 Bay Area pricing for each system done as part of a remodel. Labor here runs higher than the national average, so budget toward the upper half of each range for a two-story or a home with difficult access.

A few notes on the numbers. A rewire price reflects new wiring throughout, new outlets and switches, grounded circuits, and code-required items such as arc-fault and ground-fault protection. A repipe covers new supply lines to every fixture; PEX comes in at the lower end of the range and copper at the higher end. A panel upgrade from 100 to 200 amps is often bundled with a rewire and sometimes triggers a utility service upgrade with PG&E that adds cost and lead time. When all three are done together during a remodel, you save meaningfully versus three separate jobs, because the demolition and patching happen once. Signs you need it

You do not need to open a wall to spot the warning signs. On the electrical side, watch for lights that flicker or dim when an appliance kicks on, outlets or switch plates that feel warm, breakers that trip often, a fuse box rather than breakers, two-prong ungrounded outlets, and any visible cloth-sheathed or knob-and-tube wiring in the attic or basement. A burning smell near an outlet is a reason to call an electrician the same day.

On the plumbing side, the tells are low or dropping water pressure, water that runs rusty or discolored after sitting, visible corrosion or mineral crust on exposed pipe, recurring pinhole leaks, and pipe you can identify as galvanized steel or polybutylene. Any single sign is worth investigating; several together in a home built before the 1980s is a strong indication that a rewire or repipe belongs in the remodel scope. Catching these systems in planning, rather than mid-build, is one of the themes in our Bay Area home remodel guide, and it is exactly the kind of item a thorough architecture and design phase flags before construction begins.

Permits and inspections

Rewiring, repiping, and panel upgrades are all permitted work in every Bay Area jurisdiction. A whole-house rewire and a panel change require an electrical permit; a repipe requires a plumbing permit. Your licensed contractor pulls these permits from the city or county building department as part of the project, and the work is inspected in two stages: a rough inspection while the walls are open, verifying that new wire and pipe are installed correctly before anything is covered, and a final inspection once the systems are complete and the walls are closed.

The rough inspection is the reason sequencing matters. Wire and pipe have to be inspected before drywall goes back up, so this work is scheduled early in the build, right after demolition and any structural changes. Skipping permits to save time is a false economy: unpermitted electrical and plumbing work creates safety liability, can void insurance, and reliably surfaces during a home sale, where it becomes a costly problem to correct after the fact. A design-build team that handles permitting keeps this on track so inspections do not stall the schedule. UniqHaus holds CSLB #1158697 and manages permits and inspections as part of the remodel.

Why it is far easier and cheaper during a remodel

Here is the core reason to bundle this work into a whole house remodel: the expensive part of rewiring and repiping is not the wire or the pipe, it is opening and repairing finished walls. On an occupied home with finished surfaces, an electrician or plumber has to cut into drywall, fish new lines through wall cavities blindly, then patch, texture, prime, and paint every opening. That demolition and restoration can equal or exceed the cost of the systems themselves, and it turns a clean job into a dusty, disruptive one.

During a whole house remodel, those walls, floors, and ceilings are already open. Crews run new circuits and new supply lines with full, clear access, place outlets and fixtures exactly where the new design wants them, and there is no separate patch-and-paint bill because the walls were going to be finished anyway. You pay for the systems, not for tearing your house apart twice. Combining the work into one permitted project also means one mobilization, one round of rough inspections, and one schedule instead of two separate disruptive jobs months apart. That is why homeowners who rewire or repipe during a remodel consistently spend less, in total, than those who do it later as a standalone project. Frequently Asked Questions

When does a whole house remodel need rewiring or repiping?

A remodel needs rewiring or repiping when the home still runs on outdated systems that are unsafe or failing: knob-and-tube or aluminum branch wiring, galvanized steel or polybutylene supply pipe, or an undersized electrical panel that cannot carry modern loads. If your Bay Area home was built before the 1970s and has never had these systems replaced, plan for the work as part of the remodel budget.

How much does it cost to rewire and repipe a house in 2026?

In the 2026 Bay Area, a whole-house rewire runs roughly $14,000 to $45,000 depending on home size and access, a whole-house repipe runs about $9,000 to $35,000, and a panel upgrade from 100 to 200 amps runs about $3,500 to $8,000. A small home sits near the low end and a large two-story near the high end. Doing all three during a remodel while walls are open removes most of the demolition and patch cost.

What are the signs a house needs rewiring or repiping?

Warning signs include flickering lights, warm outlets, a fuse box or two-prong outlets, frequent breaker trips, and cloth or knob-and-tube wiring. For plumbing, watch for low water pressure, rusty or discolored water, recurring leaks, and galvanized or polybutylene pipe. Any of these in an older Bay Area home is a strong reason to rewire or repipe during the remodel.

Do you need permits to rewire or repipe a house?

Yes. A whole-house rewire, a repipe, and a panel upgrade each require a permit from your city or county building department, and the work must pass a rough inspection while the walls are open and a final inspection before closeup. A licensed contractor pulls the permits and schedules the inspections. Skipping permits creates safety and resale problems later, so this is not a step to cut.

Why is it easier to rewire or repipe during a remodel?

Because the biggest hidden cost of rewiring or repiping is opening and repairing finished walls. During a whole house remodel the walls, floors, and ceilings are already open, so crews run new wire and pipe with clear access and you pay for the systems, not for demolition and patching twice. Combining the work into one permitted project also means one round of inspections and one schedule instead of two disruptive jobs.

Key Takeaways

  • Rewire or repipe when the home has knob-and-tube or aluminum wiring, galvanized or polybutylene pipe, or an undersized panel - common in pre-1970s Bay Area homes.
  • Budget roughly $14,000 to $45,000 to rewire, $9,000 to $35,000 to repipe, and $3,500 to $8,000 for a 200-amp panel upgrade in 2026, scaled to home size.
  • Watch for flickering lights, warm outlets, low water pressure, and rusty water; these are the visible signs of failing systems behind the walls.
  • Doing the work during a remodel, while walls are open, avoids paying to demo and patch twice and keeps it to one permit and one inspection round.
