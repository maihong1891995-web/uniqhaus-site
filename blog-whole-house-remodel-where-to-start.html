The most expensive mistake on a whole house remodel is starting in the wrong place. Homeowners are eager to pick tile and paint, but the real first steps happen long before demolition - and the order of the work that follows is set by construction logic, not by which room excites you most. This guide lays out exactly where to start a whole house remodel and the right order of operations, so the money you spend stays in the finished home instead of getting torn out. It pairs with our whole house remodel checklist and the whole house remodel service.

Where to start: before you touch a wall

A whole house remodel starts with decisions, not demolition. Four things come before any physical work, in this order:

  • 1. Define your goals. Write down how you want to live in the home - the problems to solve, the rooms that matter most, and the must-haves versus nice-to-haves. This is what every later decision gets measured against.
  • 2. Set a realistic budget. Anchor to real Bay Area numbers and hold a 10-20% contingency. Our whole house remodel cost guide gives the ranges by level and home size.
  • 3. Inspect the home. This is the single most important physical step, and it comes first. Knowing the true condition of the foundation, framing, wiring, and plumbing decides what is possible and what it will cost - especially in older pre-1980 Bay Area homes.
  • 4. Design and draw the plans. Turn goals plus site reality into a design, then construction drawings, then permits. Seeing the home in 3D before demo is how choices get made deliberately - the core of our design package.

If the inspection reveals a failing structure, this is also the moment the remodel-versus-rebuild question comes up - our remodel vs rebuild guide covers that fork. Getting these four right is 90% of a smooth project, because every problem is cheaper to solve on paper than in the field.

The right order of operations

Once plans and permits are in hand, construction follows a fixed sequence that works from the structure outward. Each phase has to be complete before the next begins, because later phases cover up the earlier ones.

PhaseWhat happensWhy this order
1. DemolitionRemove finishes and anything being replacedExposes the structure and systems to work on
2. StructureFoundation, framing, load-bearing changes, additionsEverything else attaches to a sound structure
3. Rough systemsElectrical, plumbing, HVAC in open wallsMust be in before walls are closed and inspected
4. Insulation & drywallClose the walls after rough inspections passOnly after systems are approved
5. FinishesFlooring, cabinets, tile, paint, fixturesThe visible layer goes on last, over finished walls
6. Final & punch listFinal inspection, touch-ups, closeoutSign-off before you move back in

The logic is simple: anything that gets covered up has to go in first. Wiring and plumbing live inside walls, so they precede drywall; drywall precedes paint; subfloor precedes flooring. Inspections gate the sequence too - rough electrical and plumbing must pass before insulation and drywall close the walls. This is why a whole house remodel cannot be reordered to "do the pretty parts first."

Which room to remodel first

On a true whole house remodel, the work is organized by phase across the whole home, not room by room - the framing crew frames everywhere, the electricians wire everywhere, and finishes go in as each area is ready. Asking which room to do first mostly applies when you are phasing a large project over time for budget reasons.

If you do need to phase, a practical order is:

  • Structural and systems-tied areas first. Anything that touches the foundation, load-bearing walls, or a full rewire and repipe should lead, because it is disruptive and everything else depends on it.
  • The kitchen next. It is the most complex and highest-value room, and it often ties into the plumbing and electrical work already underway.
  • Bathrooms alongside the systems work. They share the plumbing scope, so grouping them is efficient.
  • Self-contained cosmetic rooms last. Bedrooms and living spaces that only need paint, flooring, and finishes are the easiest to defer without redoing work.

Phasing always costs more than doing the whole house at once, because crews mobilize and demobilize repeatedly - so phase only when the budget genuinely requires it.

Common order-of-operations mistakes

Most remodel regret traces back to sequence, not taste. The costly ones:

  • Buying finishes before the design is final. Tile and fixtures bought early often do not fit the final plan, and they tie up money you need for surprises.
  • Skipping the inspection. Repainting over knob-and-tube wiring or corroded plumbing means tearing out new finishes to fix what should have been handled first.
  • Starting demolition without permits. A whole house remodel almost always needs permits for structural, electrical, plumbing, or layout changes, filed at SJPermits.org; work done without them can be red-tagged and redone.
  • Treating design as optional. Design and architecture typically run 8-15% of the budget and prevent far more expensive mid-build changes.
  • No contingency. Walls open up and reveal surprises; a 10-20% reserve is what keeps the sequence moving instead of stalling.

A design-build team avoids most of these by making the plan, the permit path, and the build one continuous process - the same people who design and price the work also sequence and build it. That is exactly how our architecture and permitting and construction work fit together.

How long each phase takes

Before construction, design, engineering, and permitting typically take a couple of months, and permitting with the City of San Jose is the variable that moves the schedule most. That is why we start drawings and submittal in parallel rather than waiting for one to finish before the other begins. A whole house remodel then runs about 4 to 9 months of construction, and a full down-to-studs gut takes longer. For the phase-by-phase schedule, see our construction and remodel timeline.

Frequently Asked Questions

Where do I start with a whole house remodel?

Start with goals and a budget, then a full inspection of the home's structure and systems, then design. The most common mistake is starting with demolition or finishes; the first real step is planning, because the condition of the foundation, wiring, and plumbing decides what is possible and what it will cost.

What should you do first when remodeling a house?

Before touching a wall, define your goals and budget, get the home inspected, and complete a design and set of plans. Inspection comes first among the physical steps because knowing the true condition of the foundation, wiring, and plumbing prevents the mid-project surprises that blow past a budget.

In what order should I remodel my house?

Work from the structure outward: foundation and framing first, then rough systems (electrical, plumbing, HVAC), then insulation and drywall, then finishes (flooring, cabinets, paint, fixtures). Doing cosmetics before structure and systems is wasted money, because finished surfaces get torn out to fix what is behind them.

Which room should I remodel first?

On a whole house remodel the order is set by construction logic, not room preference - structure and systems are handled across the whole home before any room is finished. If you must phase the work, tackle the kitchen and the areas tied to structural or systems changes first, and leave self-contained cosmetic rooms for last.

How long does the planning phase take before construction?

Design, engineering, and permitting typically take a couple of months before construction begins, and permitting with the City of San Jose is the variable that moves the schedule most. A whole house remodel runs about 4 to 9 months in total, so front-loading design and submittal in parallel is how you protect the timeline.

Key Takeaways

  • Start with goals, budget, inspection, and design - not demolition or finishes. Planning is the real first step.
  • Construction runs from the structure outward: demolition, structure, rough systems, insulation and drywall, finishes, final.
  • Anything that gets covered up goes in first - wiring and plumbing before drywall, drywall before paint - and inspections gate each phase.
  • Phase room by room only when the budget requires it; doing the whole house at once is cheaper than repeated mobilizations.
