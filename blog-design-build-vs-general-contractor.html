Before you get a single price on a remodel, addition, or custom home, you make one decision that shapes the whole project: how you deliver it. The two main paths are design-build - one team handling both the design and the construction under one contract - and the traditional route of hiring an architect first, then handing the finished plans to a separate general contractor. This is the pillar guide to that choice: what each method actually is, how they compare on accountability, speed, cost, and change orders, when a general contractor is genuinely the better fit, and how to hire safely in California. For the deeper dives, branch off to what a design-build firm actually does and our full checklist on how to choose a contractor in the Bay Area.

Design-build vs general contractor: what is the difference?

The core difference is scope: design-build is one team and one contract for both the design and the construction, while a general contractor only builds - working from plans that a separate architect already drew. In the traditional path, called design-bid-build, you hire an architect to design the project, then competitively bid the finished plans out to separate general contractors and sign a second contract with the builder you pick. A design-build firm collapses those two relationships into one: the people who design your project are the same people who build it.

That single distinction drives everything else. With a general contractor, design and construction are two contracts with two companies, so if the drawings and the built reality do not line up, the architect and the builder can each point at the other. With design-build, there is one point of accountability for the whole outcome. A general contractor is the right partner when the design is already settled; a design-build studio is built for projects where design and construction need to be solved together.

Design-build vs design-bid-build - side by side

The clearest way to see the trade-offs is to line the two delivery methods up against each other on the things that actually affect your project - who is accountable, how the schedule runs, when you know the real cost, and how change orders tend to play out.

FactorDesign-Build (one team)Design-Bid-Build + GC (traditional)
ContractsOne contract for design and constructionTwo - architect first, then a separate builder

None of this makes design-bid-build wrong - it makes it a different tool. If you value putting one finished design out to several builders and comparing hard numbers, the traditional split does exactly that. If you value one team owning the result from first sketch to final inspection, design-build does that. The sections below unpack the two factors homeowners ask about most: speed and cost.

Which is faster and which controls cost better?

Design-build is usually faster and gives earlier cost certainty, because it overlaps phases and removes the second bidding round. In design-bid-build, the steps run one after another: the architect finishes the drawings, then you send them out to bid, then you wait for numbers to come back, then the winning builder starts. In design-build, permitting, procurement, and long-lead ordering can begin while design is still being finalized, so construction starts sooner. It also means you learn the real number earlier - the builder is pricing the project as it is being designed, not reacting to it months later.

Cost certainty is really about change orders. The single most common reason a project blows past its budget is a change order: something in the plans could not be built as drawn, or a real-world condition forces a redesign mid-construction. Because a design-build team's builder helped create the plans, those conflicts get caught on paper instead of after the walls are open - which is the mechanism behind "fewer change orders." Design and architecture fees typically run 8-15% of project cost either way, so the delivery method is not about avoiding design fees; it is about how reliably the rest of the budget holds. Whichever route you take, permitting drives a big share of the calendar - see our permitting service and how it fits the overall timeline below.

Overlapping those permit weeks with design and ordering is exactly where the design-build path saves calendar time on larger projects like custom homes and additions.

When a general contractor is the right choice

A standalone general contractor is the right call in three clear situations. First, when you already have complete, permit-ready plans from an architect you want to keep working with - the design decisions are made, so you only need someone to build them well. Second, when you specifically want to competitively bid a finished design to several builders and compare hard numbers on identical drawings. Third, for a straightforward, like-for-like job - swapping fixtures or finishes in the same footprint - where there is no real design work to do and no walls or systems moving.

In those cases the split delivery method plays to its strengths. Where a general-contractor-only approach gets riskier is on projects that still need meaningful design or that change scope as they go: the more decisions are still open, the more the two-contract structure exposes you to the architect-versus-builder gap. If your project is mostly build and barely design, hire a GC. If it is genuinely both, the one-team model tends to serve you better - which is what the rest of this guide, and our explainer on the design-build model, walk through.

How to hire: what to verify (license, insurance, contract)

Whichever method you choose, the vetting is the same - and in California several of these checks are matters of law, not preference. Before you sign anything, verify each of the following. The single most important item: an active CSLB license from the California State License Board, which is required for any job of $500 or more in combined labor and materials.

Two rules protect your money. California caps the down payment on a home-improvement contract at 10% of the contract price or $1,000, whichever is less - a contractor demanding more up front is a red flag. And the contractor, not the owner, should pull the permits; a contractor who asks you to pull them yourself is usually trying to shift liability. Good questions to ask any candidate: the CSLB license number, proof of insurance and workers' comp, similar completed projects, a realistic timeline, the payment schedule, who pulls permits, which work is subcontracted, the warranty, and the change-order process. How the UniqHaus design-build process works

UniqHaus is a design-build studio in San Jose, which means architecture, interior design, 3D visualization, permitting, and construction all live under one roof and one contract. In practice a project moves through a clear sequence of phases: design, then permitting, then site prep and foundation, framing, MEP rough-in (mechanical, electrical, and plumbing), insulation and drywall, finishes, and finally inspections and sign-off. Because the same team owns every phase, the drawings, the permit set, and the built result stay in agreement instead of drifting apart at each handoff.

The 3D visualization step matters more than it sounds: seeing the design before construction is where change orders get eliminated, because you approve a photoreal version of the finished space rather than discovering surprises on site. Permitting runs in parallel - we file in San Jose at SJPermits.org, route structural or square-footage-adding work through plan review, and build to the 2025 California Building Codes that have been in effect since January 1, 2026. From there the build follows the phase sequence above, with inspections during construction and a final sign-off at the end. It is the same one-team model whether the project is a remodel, an addition, an ADU, or a ground-up home in San Jose, Sunnyvale, or Cupertino.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between design-build and a general contractor?

Design-build is one team under one contract for both the design and the construction. A general contractor only builds, working from plans a separate architect already drew - the traditional design-bid-build path. Design-build gives you a single point of accountability; hiring a GC keeps design and construction as two separate contracts.

Is design-build faster than design-bid-build?

Usually yes. Design-build overlaps design, permitting, and procurement instead of running them one after another, and there is no second bidding round after the plans are finished. That compression is why a design-build team often starts construction sooner than the design-bid-build path.

When should I hire a general contractor instead of a design-build firm?

A general contractor is the right choice when you already have complete, permit-ready plans from an architect you want to keep, when you want to competitively bid a finished design to several builders, or for a straightforward like-for-like job that needs no real design work. In those cases the design decisions are already made and you only need someone to build.

What should I verify before hiring a contractor in California?

Verify an active CSLB license (required for any job of $500 or more in combined labor and materials), general liability insurance, workers' comp, bonded status, references, and a written contract. The contractor - not you - should pull the permits.

How much of a down payment can a contractor ask for in California?

California law caps the down payment on a home-improvement contract at 10% of the contract price or $1,000, whichever is less. Any contractor asking for more than that up front is a red flag.

Does the contractor or the homeowner pull the permit?

The contractor should pull the permits, not the homeowner. In San Jose, permits are filed at SJPermits.org; simple like-for-like work can be over-the-counter, while structural work or added square footage goes through plan review. The 2025 California Building Codes have been in effect since January 1, 2026.

Key Takeaways

  • Design-build is one team and one contract for design and construction; a general contractor only builds from plans a separate architect drew.
  • Design-build's advantages are a single point of accountability, overlapping schedules that start construction sooner, earlier cost certainty, and fewer change orders.
  • A general contractor is the right fit when you already have permit-ready plans, want to competitively bid a finished design, or have a simple like-for-like job.
  • Always verify an active CSLB license (required for jobs $500+), insurance, workers' comp, and a written contract - and remember California caps the down payment at 10% or $1,000, whichever is less.
