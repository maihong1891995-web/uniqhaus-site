What Does a Design-Build Firm Actually Do?

A design-build firm delivers both the design and the construction of a project under one team and one contract - architecture, interior design, 3D visualization, permitting, and construction from the same studio, giving you a single point of accountability from first sketch to final sign-off.

If you have started researching a remodel, an addition, or a new home, you have run into the term "design-build" - usually without a clear explanation of what it actually means. In plain terms, a design-build firm holds one contract for both the design and the construction of your project, so the people who draw it are the same people who build it. That is the opposite of the traditional path, where you hire an architect first and then bid the finished plans out to separate contractors. This guide explains exactly what falls under one roof, how the process runs step by step, and where the model helps most. For a head-to-head on delivery methods, our design-build vs general contractor breakdown is the deeper comparison.

What is a design-build firm?

A design-build firm is a single studio that takes a project from concept through completed construction under one team and one contract. Instead of coordinating a separate architect, interior designer, and general contractor yourself, you sign with one company that carries all of those roles. A general contractor, by contrast, builds from someone else's plans; an architect draws plans someone else builds. A design-build firm does both the design and the build.

The practical payoff is a single point of accountability. When one team owns the drawings, the budget, the permit, and the crew, there is no gap to fall through and no finger-pointing when a detail on the plans meets reality on site. It also means design, permitting, and procurement can overlap rather than run one-after-another, which is where the schedule and cost benefits come from. What services a design-build firm handles

A full-service design-build studio covers the entire arc of a project - the creative, the technical, and the physical work - so nothing gets subcontracted out to a firm you never chose. The table below shows what typically lives under one roof and what each part actually delivers.

ServiceWhat it delivers
ArchitectureFloor plans, structural design, and code-compliant construction drawings
Interior designLayouts, finishes, fixtures, lighting, and material selections
3D visualizationPhotoreal renderings so you approve the look before anything is built
PermittingFiling at SJPermits.org, managing plan review, and scheduling inspections
ConstructionSite prep, framing, MEP, finishes, and final sign-off with an in-house crew

The value of bundling these is that decisions made in design are priced and built by the same team - a finish chosen in the architecture and design phase is one the builder has already costed. Permitting in particular benefits: the studio that drew the plans is the one that files them, so there is no lag while a contractor learns a set of drawings they did not create. The design-build process, step by step

Most design-build projects follow the same five-stage arc, with each stage feeding the next. Because one team runs all of it, the stages overlap where they can - material ordering starts during permitting, for example - rather than waiting in a strict line.

  1. Design. The team develops floor plans, structural design, interiors, and 3D renderings so you sign off on the look and layout before construction dollars are committed.
  2. Permitting. The firm files the plans at SJPermits.org. Simple like-for-like work can be over-the-counter; anything structural or that adds square footage goes through plan review.
  3. Site prep and foundation. Demolition, grading, and the foundation - the physical starting line once permits are in hand.
  4. Framing and MEP rough-in. The structure goes up, then mechanical, electrical, and plumbing are roughed in and inspected inside the walls.
  5. Finishes, inspections, and final sign-off. Insulation and drywall, then finishes, then the inspections that lead to a final sign-off from the city.

How long that arc takes depends entirely on scope. Permit review alone runs from same-day over-the-counter approval up to a few months for new construction, and construction timelines vary widely by project type. The table below shows typical design-to-done durations.

Project typeTypical timeline (design to done)
Bathroom remodel8-14 weeks
Kitchen remodel12-20 weeks
Home addition4-8 months
Whole-house remodel4-9 months
ADU6-12 months
Custom home12-24 months

Permit timing sits inside those windows: over-the-counter issuance runs same day to about a week, residential plan review commonly 3-8 weeks, and additions or new construction 2-4 months. Note that the 2025 California Building Codes have been in effect since January 1, 2026, so plans must meet current standards. Design-build vs hiring an architect and a contractor separately

The traditional alternative is called design-bid-build: you hire an architect, get finished plans, then competitively bid those plans out to separate general contractors. That path can work well and it makes competitive bidding easy, but it splits responsibility between two firms and pushes real pricing to the very end - after the design is already locked. Design-build keeps design and construction in one contract so the builder is pricing the work as it is drawn.

FactorDesign-buildDesign-bid-build (traditional)
ContractsOne team, one contractSeparate architect and contractor
AccountabilitySingle point of accountabilitySplit between two firms
Cost certaintyEarlier - builder prices as it is designedLater - real bids come after plans are done
Change ordersFewer - the builder helped design itMore common at the design-to-build handoff
ScheduleDesign, permitting, and procurement overlapLargely sequential

Neither model changes the design fee much - architecture and design fees typically run 8-15% of project cost either way. The difference is where surprises land: design-build front-loads cost certainty and tends to produce fewer change orders because the builder helped design the project, while design-bid-build defers hard numbers until the bid. Who should choose design-build?

Design-build fits homeowners who want one accountable partner and early cost certainty rather than the lowest possible bid on a fixed set of plans. If your project involves structural change, added square footage, or coordinated design and construction - a kitchen, an addition, an ADU, a whole-house remodel, or a custom home - the overlap between design, permitting, and building is where the model earns its keep. It is also the right fit if you would rather not personally manage the handoff between a designer and a builder.

Whichever path you choose, vetting the firm is the same job. In California you should verify an active CSLB license (required for any job of $500 or more in combined labor and materials), general liability insurance, workers' comp, bonded status, references, and a written contract - and the contractor, not you, should pull the permits. California law also caps the down payment on a home-improvement contract at 10% of the price or $1,000, whichever is less. Frequently Asked Questions

What is a design-build firm?

A design-build firm delivers both the design and the construction of a project under one team and one contract. That means architecture, interior design, 3D visualization, permitting, and construction all come from the same studio, giving you a single point of accountability from first sketch to final sign-off.

What is the difference between design-build and design-bid-build?

Design-build means one team holds one contract for both the design and the build. Design-bid-build is the traditional path: you hire an architect, then competitively bid the finished plans out to separate general contractors. Design-build removes the handoff between two firms.

Is design-build cheaper than hiring an architect and contractor separately?

Design-build gives earlier cost certainty and usually fewer change orders, because the builder helps design the project and prices it as it is drawn. Design and architecture fees typically run 8-15% of project cost in either model, so the savings come mainly from fewer surprises, not a lower design fee.

Who pulls the permits with a design-build firm?

The contractor should pull the permits, not the owner. With a design-build firm the same studio that drew the plans files them at SJPermits.org, manages plan review, and schedules the inspections through construction and final sign-off.

Is design-build faster than the traditional path?

Often, yes. Because design, permitting, and procurement overlap when one team runs them, a design-build studio can compress the calendar versus handing off between a separate designer and contractor. Actual duration still depends on scope - a bathroom runs 8-14 weeks while a custom home runs 12-24 months.

Key Takeaways

  • Design-build means one team and one contract for both design and construction - architecture, interior design, 3D visualization, permitting, and construction under one roof.
  • The traditional alternative, design-bid-build, hires an architect first and then bids the finished plans to separate general contractors, splitting responsibility between two firms.
  • The model's advantages are a single point of accountability, overlapping design and permitting and procurement, earlier cost certainty, and fewer change orders because the builder helped design it.
  • In California, always verify an active CSLB license, insurance, workers' comp, bonded status, and a written contract - and the contractor, not you, should pull the permits.
