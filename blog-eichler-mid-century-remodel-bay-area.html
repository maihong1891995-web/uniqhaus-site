Remodeling an Eichler or Mid-Century Home in the Bay Area

Remodeling an Eichler or mid-century home in the Bay Area typically runs $200 to $450+ per square foot, because the work balances preserving the post-and-beam architecture with upgrading insulation, electrical, radiant heat, and the single-pane glass walls that define these homes. Silicon Valley has large Eichler tracts in areas like Palo Alto, Sunnyvale, and San Jose.

An Eichler is not a normal house, and remodeling one like a normal house is how the architecture gets ruined and the budget gets blown. Joseph Eichler built roughly 11,000 homes in California between 1949 and 1974, most of them right here in Silicon Valley, and their post-and-beam frames, glass walls, and indoor-outdoor flow are exactly what buyers pay a premium for today. The same features that make them beautiful also make them tricky to upgrade. This guide explains what makes an Eichler or mid-century modern home remodel different, what it costs in the Bay Area, and how to modernize comfort and efficiency without erasing the design. It pairs with our whole house remodel service and our architecture and permitting work.

What makes an Eichler or mid-century home different to remodel

The short answer: almost every standard remodel assumption breaks down in these homes. Where a typical house gives you an attic, a pitched roof, and stud walls with cavities to work in, an Eichler gives you none of that. The defining features are:

  • Post-and-beam framing. Exposed beams sit on posts and carry the roof directly, so the walls are often not load-bearing - but the beams and posts are, which changes how you open up space.
  • Flat or low-slope roofs with no attic. There is no roof cavity to hide ducts, add insulation, or run new wiring, so those upgrades have to be solved at the roof or in the slab.
  • Slab-on-grade with radiant heat. The floor is a concrete slab, and heating usually runs through pipes cast into that slab. There is no crawlspace under most of the home.
  • Floor-to-ceiling glass and atriums. Single-pane glass walls and open atriums are the heart of the design and the biggest source of heat loss.
  • Exposed tongue-and-groove ceilings. The underside of the roof deck is the finished ceiling, so there is no hidden space above the living areas.

Because the beams, glass, and ceilings are all visible and structural at once, you cannot simply gut and rebuild the way you would a 1990s tract home. The design and the structure are the same thing. That is why an Eichler remodel starts with a plan that respects the module, and why seeing it first in 3D visualization before demolition is so valuable.

Common upgrade challenges

Most of the cost and complexity in a mid-century remodel comes from a handful of predictable issues. Nearly all of them trace back to the same root: these homes were built for a milder view of comfort and energy than we expect in 2026. Here is what comes up on almost every project and how it is typically solved.

FeatureIssueTypical upgrade
Single-pane glass wallsMajor heat loss and glareHigh-performance dual-pane glazing in slim frames
Radiant slab heatingHard to modify or repair once cast in the slabCareful planning; repair, zone, or supplement the system
Flat roofProne to leaks and holds little insulationRe-roof with added insulation above the deck
Original electricalUndersized panel and wiring for modern loadsRewire and install a new panel and service
InsulationLittle to none in roof and wallsRoof and wall insulation added where accessible
Exposed post-and-beamStructural changes are tricky and visibleStructural engineer required for any beam or post change

The theme across the table is that comfort and efficiency upgrades are worth doing but rarely simple, because there is no attic or wall cavity to hide the work in. Handled well, an Eichler can be quiet, warm, and efficient while still looking exactly like an Eichler. Handled poorly - lowered ceilings, chunky window frames, a bulked-up roofline - the character disappears. This is where design and construction under one team pays off.

How much does an Eichler remodel cost?

A mid-century or Eichler remodel in the Bay Area typically runs $200 to $450 or more per square foot, with mid-range whole-home work landing around $200 to $400 per square foot and character-sensitive, high-performance projects pushing toward the top of the range. That sits above a basic cosmetic remodel for a clear reason: you are paying for performance upgrades that a newer home already has.

The premium comes from the items in the table above - new glazing, rewiring, added insulation, a new roof, and any radiant-heat work - plus the extra care and engineering needed to make structural or layout changes without spoiling the post-and-beam look. For the full picture on whole-home pricing and what drives it, see our whole house remodel cost guide. If an inspection turns up a failing slab or widespread structural issues, it is also worth reading our remodel vs rebuild guide, though with an Eichler the architectural value almost always argues for a sensitive remodel over a teardown.

Preserving character while modernizing

The goal of a good Eichler remodel is simple to state and hard to execute: keep everything that makes it an Eichler, and quietly fix everything that makes it uncomfortable. In practice that means protecting three things above all.

  • Keep the beams and ceilings exposed. Never drop a ceiling to hide ducts or insulation. Solve those needs at the roof instead, so the tongue-and-groove ceiling and beam lines stay intact.
  • Keep the glass and the light. Replace single-pane walls with high-performance glazing in slim, period-appropriate frames rather than bulky modern units, so the walls still read as walls of glass.
  • Keep the indoor-outdoor flow. The atrium, the sliders, and the connection to the yard are central to the design. Modern doors and glass can preserve that relationship while sealing it against drafts.

Kitchens and baths are where updating is most welcome, and they can be brought fully up to date while staying sympathetic to the era - flat-panel cabinetry, honest materials, clean lines, and warm wood tones that echo the ceilings. Energy performance is layered in through better glass, roof insulation, air sealing, and updated systems rather than visible bulk. Done this way, the home performs like new construction and still looks like the home Eichler drew. Frequently Asked Questions

How much does it cost to remodel an Eichler home?

An Eichler or mid-century remodel in the Bay Area typically runs $200 to $450 or more per square foot, with mid-range whole-home work landing around $200 to $400 per square foot. The premium over a basic cosmetic remodel comes from performance upgrades - insulation, rewiring, high-performance glazing, and re-roofing - done without wrecking the post-and-beam character.

What is special about remodeling a mid-century home?

Mid-century and Eichler homes use post-and-beam framing, flat or low-slope roofs with no attic, a slab-on-grade floor with radiant heat, and floor-to-ceiling single-pane glass. Those features mean standard remodel assumptions do not apply - there is no attic to run ducts or add insulation, the slab hides the heating, and the glass walls and exposed beams are the architecture, so they have to be preserved rather than covered.

Can you add insulation to an Eichler?

Yes. Because there is no attic, insulation is added at the roof - either above the deck during a re-roof or with a new insulated roof assembly - along with wall insulation where walls are opened up. The goal is to raise comfort and energy performance while keeping the low rooflines and exposed beams intact.

Where are Eichler homes in the Bay Area?

Silicon Valley has some of the largest Eichler tracts in the country, concentrated in Palo Alto, Sunnyvale, and San Jose, with more mid-century neighborhoods across the Peninsula and South Bay. That is why local design-build experience with these homes matters - the housing stock and the permitting are both regional.

Can you change the layout of a post-and-beam home?

Yes, but it takes planning. In a post-and-beam home the beams and posts often carry the roof, so removing or moving them requires a structural engineer and usually new beams or supports. Many layout goals - opening a kitchen, reworking a bath - can be met by working with the module rather than against it, which keeps costs and risk down.

Key Takeaways

  • Eichler and mid-century homes are defined by post-and-beam framing, flat roofs with no attic, radiant slabs, and glass walls - standard remodel assumptions do not apply.
  • Expect roughly $200 to $450+ per square foot; the premium over a cosmetic remodel pays for insulation, rewiring, new glazing, and re-roofing.
  • Modernize comfort and efficiency at the roof and in the glass, never by dropping ceilings or hiding the beams that make the home an Eichler.
  • Use a design-build team with post-and-beam and Bay Area permitting experience so structural, energy, and design choices are made together.
