Aging in Place Remodel: A Room-by-Room Plan for Bay Area Homes

An aging in place remodel puts a bedroom and full bath on the main floor, creates one step-free entrance, and uses 36-inch doors (about 32 inches clear), a curbless shower, and grab bar blocking. Build the hidden parts, like blocking, wiring, and the shower floor recess, during your remodel. Adding them later means opening finished walls and floors.

An aging in place remodel plans your home so it works at 45, at 75, and for a parent who moves in next year. For Bay Area homeowners who love their neighborhood and their Prop 13 tax base, it is often the most practical way to stay put: you fix the house once, while the walls are already open, instead of retrofitting in a hurry later.

You are not alone in wanting this. AARP's 2024 Home and Community Preferences Survey found that 75 percent of adults 50 and older want to stay in their current homes as they age. This guide walks through each room, what to change, and which changes are much easier to make during a remodel than as a later retrofit.

What is an aging in place remodel, and how is it different from universal design?

An aging in place remodel removes the barriers that make a home harder to use as bodies change: steps, narrow doors, tubs you climb into, dim stairs, and knobs that need a strong grip. Universal design is the broader idea behind it. It aims for a home that is easier for everyone, from a toddler to a grandparent to someone on crutches after knee surgery, without looking clinical.

The safety case is real. The CDC reports that more than one out of four older people falls each year. Most of the fixes that reduce that risk are quiet design choices: level floors, good light, solid handholds, and space to move.

A useful starting benchmark is visitability, a concept promoted by the advocacy group Concrete Change. A visitable home has three basics: one zero-step entrance, doors with 32 inches of clear passage, and at least a half bath on the main floor. If your remodel achieves those three, a parent using a walker can visit, and you have a foundation to build on.

For more detail, two free and credible references are worth bookmarking:

  • The AARP HomeFit Guide, an illustrated room-by-room guide with tips for existing houses.
  • The federal 2010 ADA Standards for Accessible Design, published by the U.S. Access Board.

One important point: the ADA Standards apply to public and commercial buildings, not to most private single-family homes. We use their dimensions below as reference numbers, because they are tested and well documented. They are guidance for your home, not legal requirements, and your designer should fit them to the people who will actually live there.

Room by room: what should change, and when?

The short answer: most aging in place changes cost far less when they are built into a remodel than when they are added later. The table below shows the key changes for each area, why they matter, and whether they are best done during a remodel.

Notice the pattern in the last column. Almost every item depends on framing, plumbing, or wiring. That is why the best time to plan for later is during the remodel you are already doing.

Which aging in place changes should you make while the walls are open?

Four items are inexpensive during construction and disruptive to add afterward: blocking, wider doors, curbless shower prep, and wiring. If you do nothing else, do these.

1. Blocking for grab bars and handrails

Blocking is solid wood (or a backing panel) installed between studs so a grab bar can be anchored anywhere you need it. The ADA reference is a bar that can resist a 250-pound force and sits 33 to 36 inches above the floor beside a toilet or in a shower. You do not need to install bars on day one. With blocking behind the tile, you can add a stylish bar in an afternoon later, without cutting into a finished wall. We suggest blocking around the toilet, the full shower or tub area, and both sides of stairs.

2. Wider doors and openings

A standard 36-inch door gives roughly 32 inches of clear width when open, which matches the ADA reference for doors. Pocket doors and barn-style sliders help where a swing would block a small bathroom. Changing a door size during framing is a line item. Changing it after finishes are done touches structure, drywall, trim, flooring, and paint.

3. Curbless shower prep

A true curbless shower needs the floor under the shower to sit lower than the bathroom floor so water drains without a lip. On a slab, that means recessing the concrete. On a raised wood floor, it often means adjusting joists. Both are straightforward when the bathroom is gutted, which is why we plan it at the start of every bathroom remodel where it fits. Our guide to the walk-in shower vs tub decision covers the shower details, resale questions, and when to keep a tub.

4. Lighting, switches, and outlets

Open walls let you add circuits for layered lighting, motion night lights, and outlets raised off the floor at little added cost. The ADA reference for an unobstructed forward reach is 15 to 48 inches above the floor, which is a helpful range for switches, outlets, and controls. Ask for a few extra wired boxes now, such as at the stair top and bottom, so smart switches or sensors can be added later without patching walls.

A practical rule we use: if a future change would need a wall opened, decide on it during design, even if you build only the hidden part now.

How do you create single-level living in a two-story Bay Area home?

You have two main paths: convert existing main-floor space into a bedroom suite, or add square footage for one. The right choice depends on your floor plan, lot, and budget.

  • Convert what you have. A den, family room, or underused formal dining room next to a bathroom can often become a main-floor bedroom suite. This usually costs less because you are not adding foundation or roof. It works best when plumbing is nearby.
  • Add a main-floor suite. If no room can give up space, a ground-floor addition creates a bedroom, accessible bathroom, and closet without losing living area. Home additions in the Bay Area typically run $100,000 to $500,000+, depending on size, structure, and finishes. Our primary suite addition guide covers costs, permits, and when adding beats reconfiguring.
  • Plan the stairs for later. If you stay in a two-story home, rebuild stairs with rails on both sides and good light, and consider where a future stair lift or small residential elevator could fit. Reserving a stacked closet on both floors is one way designers keep that option open.

Many 1960s to 1980s ranch homes in San Jose, Sunnyvale, and Campbell are already one level. They are strong candidates for aging in place because the work is mostly inside: doors, the bathroom, lighting, and one entry.

How does an aging in place remodel fit multigenerational living?

If a parent is moving in, the real question is how much privacy each household needs. A main-floor suite inside the house keeps everyone close and is often the simplest to permit. A separate unit gives more independence for both generations.

Bay Area families usually choose among three options:

  1. Main-floor suite in the house. Bedroom, accessible bath, and a sitting area, sharing the family kitchen.
  2. In-law suite or JADU. A suite with its own entrance and a small kitchen. See our in-law suite cost guide for numbers.
  3. Detached ADU. A separate home on your lot, typically $150,000 to $400,000. Our comparison of an ADU vs home addition walks through privacy, cost, and resale for multigenerational families.

Whichever you choose, apply the same aging in place basics to the new space: a level entry, wide doors, a curbless shower, and blocking. Building them into a new unit from the start is the easiest version of all.

Which smart home features actually help?

A few simple tools help most: motion-activated lighting, video doorbells, smart locks with keypads, leak sensors near water heaters and washers, and thermostats that can be checked from a phone. They are useful when an adult child wants to check in without hovering. Keep controls simple, keep a manual backup for every lock and light, and avoid systems only one family member knows how to run. The wiring and extra boxes from the section above make all of these easy to add later.

What does this look like in real Bay Area remodels?

Two UniqHaus projects show how a remodel or addition can reshape a home around how a family wants to live for the long term.

  • Blair, Sunnyvale (2024): a full-home remodel and addition that took the house from 3 bedrooms and 1 bath to 4 bedrooms and 3.5 baths, growing from about 1,518 to about 1,918 square feet. Every room was rebuilt from the studs, which is exactly the moment when blocking, door widths, and bathroom layouts can be set for the long term.
  • Bird Ave, San Jose (2021): a major addition and whole-home remodel that grew the home from about 1,046 to about 2,207 square feet, with 4 bedrooms, 3 baths, a new porch, a rear patio, and an attached garage on a 7,405 sq ft lot.

The lesson from projects like these: when you are already adding bathrooms and rebuilding rooms, planning for later adds very little to the scope. Our home remodeling team designs, draws, permits, and builds in-house, so the details behind the walls match the plan on paper.

What does an aging in place remodel cost, and are any costs deductible?

Cost follows scope. A focused bathroom or kitchen project typically runs $30,000 to $150,000+, a whole-house remodel $100 to $500 per square foot, and a home addition $100,000 to $500,000+. Hold a 10 to 20 percent contingency, especially in older homes.

On taxes, IRS Publication 502 explains that some home improvements made to accommodate a disability, such as entrance ramps, widened doorways, and grab bars, may count as medical expenses. Medical expenses are deductible only to the extent they exceed 7.5 percent of adjusted gross income, and improvements that raise your home's value are reduced by that increase. Rules depend on your situation, so check with a tax professional before counting on a deduction.

If you are interviewing builders, you may see the Certified Aging-in-Place Specialist (CAPS) designation from the National Association of Home Builders. It shows training in aging in place design. Still ask to see how a team handles the details in real projects.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the most important aging in place modification?

For most homes it is a bedroom and full bathroom on the main floor, because it lets someone live on one level. After that, a curbless or low-threshold shower with grab bar blocking and one step-free entrance make the biggest difference.

How wide should doors be for aging in place?

A 36-inch door is a common choice because it gives roughly 32 inches of clear width when open, which matches the ADA reference for doors. The ADA Standards are guidance for private homes, not a requirement, but they are a reliable benchmark.

Does aging in place design hurt resale value?

Well-designed universal features, like wide doors, curbless showers, and good lighting, tend to read as modern and spacious rather than clinical. What hurts appeal is equipment that looks institutional, which is why blocking now and stylish grab bars later is a common approach.

Can I deduct aging in place home improvements on my taxes?

Possibly. IRS Publication 502 lists items like ramps, widened doorways, and grab bars as medical expenses when made to accommodate a disability, subject to the 7.5 percent of AGI threshold. Ask a tax professional about your situation.

Is it better to add a main-floor bedroom or build an ADU for my parents?

A main-floor suite keeps everyone under one roof and usually costs less, while an ADU gives both households more privacy. Our ADU vs home addition guide compares the two for multigenerational families.

What is blocking for grab bars?

Blocking is solid wood or backing installed inside the wall before drywall and tile, so grab bars can be anchored securely anywhere later. It is inexpensive during a remodel and avoids cutting into finished tile.

Key Takeaways

  • 75 percent of adults 50+ want to stay in their homes; one step-free entrance, 32-inch clear doors, and a main-floor bath are the baseline.
  • Install grab bar blocking during the remodel; the ADA reference is a bar 33 to 36 inches high that resists 250 pounds.
  • Use 36-inch doors and plan a 60-inch turning space in key rooms, as guidance rather than a legal requirement for homes.
  • If a future change would need a wall opened, decide it in design and build the hidden part now.
