Almost every real construction project in San Jose runs through the same gate: a building permit. It is the step homeowners understand least and worry about most, and it is where a lot of remodels stall - not because the process is impossible, but because the wrong track was chosen or the plans did not meet current code. This guide walks the whole path, from filing at SJPermits.org to the final inspector's sign-off, and explains the one distinction that decides how long you wait: over-the-counter versus plan review. It is a spoke of our design-build vs general contractor hub, and if your first question is timing, pair it with our sibling guide on how long building permits take in San Jose.

How does the building permit process work in San Jose?

The San Jose building permit process has four plain stages: you apply at SJPermits.org, the city either issues the permit over the counter or routes it to plan review, inspections happen during construction, and a final sign-off closes the permit. Which path you take depends entirely on the scope of the work. Simple like-for-like work - replacing a fixture or a water heater in the same spot - can often be issued over the counter, sometimes the same day. Anything structural, or that adds square footage, needs plan review before a permit is issued.

The reason the city cares is code compliance. As of January 1, 2026, the 2025 California Building Codes are in effect, so every new permit is reviewed against the current standard - structural details, energy, plumbing, and electrical all have to meet it. In California the contractor pulls the permit, not the owner, because the licensed builder is the party responsible for the work. Over-the-counter vs plan review - which do you need?

The single biggest question in the whole process is whether your project qualifies for an over-the-counter permit or has to go through plan review. Over-the-counter is for simple, like-for-like work and can be issued the same day to about a week. Plan review is required for anything structural or that adds square footage, and it takes longer because a city plan checker reads your drawings against the code before issuing. The table below maps typical residential work to each track.

TrackTypical workIssuance time
Over-the-counterLike-for-like fixture swaps, water heater, simple electrical or plumbing in the same location, minor non-structural workSame day to ~1 week
Plan review (residential)Structural changes, moving walls, moved plumbing or electrical, most kitchen and bath remodels with layout changesCommonly 3-8 weeks
Plan review (additions / new build)Home additions, new square footage, ADUs, custom homes2-4 months

The dividing line is almost always the same: does the work change the structure or add floor area? If yes, plan review; if no, there is a good chance it can go over the counter. This is why two projects with similar budgets can have wildly different schedules - a like-for-like bathroom refresh and a bathroom that moves the shower are on different tracks. Step by step: applying at SJPermits.org

You apply for a San Jose building permit online at SJPermits.org, the city's permit portal. The sequence is consistent whether your project is over-the-counter or headed to plan review; plan-review projects just add a drawing set and a checking cycle. Here is the path a typical residential project follows:

  1. Confirm the scope and track. Decide whether the work is like-for-like (possible over-the-counter) or structural / adds square footage (plan review). This determines everything downstream.
  2. Prepare the application and plans. Over-the-counter work needs basic project details; plan review needs a drawing set - architectural, structural, and the relevant MEP details - meeting the 2025 California Building Codes.
  3. Submit at SJPermits.org. The licensed contractor files the application. In California the contractor, not the owner, should pull the permit because the license holder is responsible for the work.
  4. Plan check (if required). A city plan checker reviews the drawings against code and may return comments to address. Cleaner, code-ready plans mean fewer correction cycles and a faster issue.
  5. Pay fees and pull the permit. Once approved, the permit is issued and construction can legally begin.

The place most homeowners lose weeks is step 4 - plans that were not drawn to current code bounce back with corrections, and each round adds time. That is the argument for having one team handle design, drawings, and permitting together, so the plans that get submitted are already built to clear review. Inspections and final sign-off

A permit is not a one-and-done document - it stays open through construction and is closed only by a final sign-off. During the build, a city inspector visits at set milestones to verify the work matches the approved plans and the 2025 California Building Codes before the next phase can cover it up. The rhythm follows the construction phases: site prep and foundation, framing, MEP rough-in (mechanical, electrical, plumbing), then insulation and drywall, and finally finishes.

The critical rule is that certain inspections must happen before work is concealed - foundation before the slab is poured, framing and rough-in before insulation and drywall go up. Miss one and the crew may have to open finished work back up, which is the single most avoidable source of delay and cost on a permitted job. When the last inspection passes, the inspector signs off and the permit is closed - the legal proof your project was built to code, which also matters at resale. Managing that inspection calendar so trades and inspectors line up is a core part of what a design-build permitting process handles for you.

What needs a permit vs what is exempt

The rule of thumb: work that is structural, adds square footage, or moves plumbing, electrical, or mechanical needs a permit, while small cosmetic work in the same footprint is often exempt. A like-for-like swap - a new toilet or faucet where the old one was, new flooring, interior paint - typically does not require one. The moment you move a wall, add a circuit, relocate a fixture, or add floor area, you are in permit territory, and usually plan review.

Usually needs a permitOften exempt (same footprint)
Moving or removing a wall (structural)Interior painting
Adding square footage or a new roomNew flooring over an existing subfloor
New or relocated electrical circuitsLike-for-like faucet, toilet, or light-fixture swap
Relocating plumbing (sink, shower, toilet)Cabinet refacing or new countertops (same layout)
HVAC / mechanical changes or a water heaterMinor drywall patching and trim
Enlarging a window or door openingReplacing a window in the same opening*

*Even a same-size window replacement can trigger a permit under current energy rules, so confirm with the city before you start. When in doubt, a quick call to San Jose Building saves a costly correction later.

Getting this call right matters because unpermitted structural or MEP work can surface at resale, fail to close a sale, or force retroactive permitting. When a project is genuinely borderline, the safe move is to confirm with the city before starting rather than after. This maps cleanly onto the kind of project you are doing: a custom home or addition is always plan review, while a cosmetic refresh may need nothing at all. Homeowners across San Jose and neighboring cities run into the same distinction, and it is the first thing we clarify on any scope. Frequently Asked Questions

How does the building permit process work in San Jose?

You apply at SJPermits.org. Simple like-for-like work can be issued over the counter, often the same day to about a week. Anything structural, or that adds square footage, goes to plan review. Once issued, inspections happen during construction and a final sign-off closes the permit.

What is the difference between over-the-counter and plan review?

Over-the-counter is for simple like-for-like work and is issued the same day to about a week. Plan review is required for anything structural or that adds square footage, and residential plan review commonly takes 3-8 weeks, with additions and new construction 2-4 months.

Who pulls the permit - the owner or the contractor?

In California, your contractor should pull the permit, not you. The contractor holds the CSLB license and is responsible for the work, so having them file the permit keeps liability where it belongs. A design-build firm handles the application and manages inspections through final sign-off.

Which building code applies to my San Jose permit in 2026?

The 2025 California Building Codes are in effect since January 1, 2026. Any permit issued now must meet the current code, so plans, structural details, and fixtures are reviewed against the 2025 standards rather than the prior cycle.

What needs a permit and what is exempt in San Jose?

Work that is structural, adds square footage, or moves plumbing, electrical, or mechanical needs a permit. Small cosmetic work like painting, flooring, or a like-for-like fixture swap in the same spot is often exempt. When in doubt, check with the city before you start.

How long does it take to get a building permit in San Jose?

An over-the-counter permit can be issued the same day to about a week. Residential plan review commonly runs 3-8 weeks, while additions and new construction take 2-4 months. The scope of the work, not the city queue alone, sets which track you are on.
