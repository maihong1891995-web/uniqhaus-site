Steps to Build a New Home on Your Lot

Building a new home on a lot you already own follows five steps: feasibility and zoning review, design and engineering, permits and plan check, construction, and final inspection and move-in. Building a new home on land you already own is one of the most rewarding projects a Bay Area homeowner can take on, and also one of the most process-heavy. Unlike buying a finished house, a new build asks you to make decisions in a specific order: what the lot allows, how the home is designed to fit it, how the city reviews and approves the plans, and how the work is sequenced on site. Skip a step or take them out of order and you risk paying for drawings a city will not approve or discovering a site constraint after the budget is set. This guide lays out the steps to build a new home on your lot in the Bay Area for a 2026 project, from the first feasibility questions through the final inspection. It pairs with our custom homes service and the deeper cost to build a custom home guide.

The steps at a glance

A new home on an existing lot moves through five clear phases. Each one has to close before the next can start, because the output of one stage becomes the input for the next: feasibility defines what you can build, design turns that into drawings, permits authorize the work, construction executes it, and final inspection releases the home for occupancy.

  1. Feasibility and zoning: Confirm the lot's zoning, allowable size, height, and setbacks, and identify site constraints before you design anything.
  2. Design: Develop the home from schematic concept through construction documents, coordinated with the engineering the site requires.
  3. Permits and plan check: Submit the drawings to your city or county, respond to plan-check corrections, and receive your building permit.
  4. Construction: Break ground and build in sequence, from site work and foundation through framing, systems, and finishes, with inspections along the way.
  5. Final and move-in: Pass the final inspection, receive approval to occupy, and complete the closeout punch list before you move in.

The sections below expand the phases where Bay Area homeowners most often need clarity. Feasibility, zoning, and site review

Every new build should begin with a feasibility and zoning review, because this is the step that tells you what you can legally and physically build on the lot. Your parcel carries a zoning designation that governs the maximum size and height of the home, the required front, side, and rear setbacks, lot coverage limits, and sometimes design or neighborhood standards. Two lots on the same street can allow very different homes, so assumptions from a neighbor's project are not a substitute for checking your own parcel.

Alongside zoning, the physical site drives cost and design more than most homeowners expect. A sloped lot, poor or expansive soils, drainage patterns, protected trees, easements, and the location of existing utility connections all shape what is buildable and what it will cost to build. In parts of the Bay Area you may also encounter geologic or fire-zone considerations that call for specific engineering or materials. Getting these facts on the table first means the design responds to the real lot rather than an idealized one, and it protects your budget from the expensive surprises that appear when a site issue is discovered late.

The practical output of this phase is a clear picture of the buildable envelope and the site work involved, which is exactly what you need before spending on full design. It is also where an experienced local team earns its keep, because reading a parcel correctly depends on knowing how a given city interprets its own rules.

Design and engineering

With feasibility settled, design turns your program and the lot's limits into a home. The work typically flows from schematic design, where the overall layout, massing, and character are established, into design development, where rooms, materials, and systems are resolved, and finally into construction documents, the detailed drawings a builder and a plan checker both rely on. Running in parallel is the engineering the project requires, most importantly structural engineering, and often civil, grading, and Title 24 energy calculations depending on the site and scope.

This is the stage where decisions have the most leverage over both cost and satisfaction. Choices about square footage, the number of stories, structural complexity, and finish level set the budget long before a single permit is pulled, which is why aligning design with a realistic number early is so important. A well-coordinated design set also smooths the next phase. When architecture, structure, and energy documents are consistent and complete, plan check tends to go faster and construction hits fewer unknowns. This coordination is a core reason many homeowners choose a design-build team for a new home: the people drawing the home and the people building it are working from the same source, so the drawings are buildable and priced against real conditions. Permits and approvals in the Bay Area

A new home requires a building permit, and in the Bay Area the details of that process are set by your specific city or county rather than a single statewide rulebook. Because each jurisdiction runs its own building department, the fees, submittal requirements, and review timelines differ from one town to the next, so the most reliable information always comes from your local department for your specific address. What follows is the general shape of the process rather than any figure presented as a guarantee.

After you submit a complete set of drawings, the city conducts a plan check, reviewing the plans against building, structural, energy, and local codes. It is normal for plan check to return one or more rounds of corrections that your design team addresses before resubmitting. Depending on the parcel, a project may also involve planning or zoning review, and some sites trigger additional steps such as design review, environmental considerations, or coordination with utility and public-works departments. More complex sites generally mean more review, which is one more reason the feasibility phase matters.

Because timelines and requirements vary so widely, the best lever a homeowner has is the quality and completeness of the submittal. A thorough, well-coordinated package tends to move through review with fewer correction cycles than a thin one, and a team that regularly permits homes in your jurisdiction knows what that department expects to see. We cover this process from the professional side in our architecture and permits work, where preparing submittals that anticipate local requirements is part of the job.

Construction and timeline

Once the permit is issued, construction proceeds in a set sequence, with city inspections at defined milestones. Site work and the foundation come first, followed by framing, then the rough-in of mechanical, electrical, and plumbing systems, then insulation and drywall, and finally interior and exterior finishes before closeout. Each major stage is followed by an inspection that must pass before the next begins, which is why a clean set of drawings and a well-run schedule matter so much to keeping the job moving.

Homeowners most often ask how long a new build takes. The honest answer is that it depends on the jurisdiction, the site, and the size and complexity of the home, so the durations below are rough planning ranges rather than promises. Weather, material lead times, plan-check cycles, and change requests all move the numbers, and a complex hillside home behaves very differently from a straightforward flat-lot build.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the steps to build a new home on your lot in the Bay Area?

The steps are feasibility and zoning review, design and engineering, permits and plan check, construction, and final inspection and move-in. On an existing lot you start by confirming what the zoning and site allow, then move into schematic and construction drawings, submit for permits, build once approvals clear, and close out with the final inspection before you occupy.

How long does it take to build a new home in the Bay Area?

It varies widely by jurisdiction and project scope. Design and engineering commonly run several months, permits and plan check add more time depending on the city and whether the site is complex, and construction of a full custom home usually spans roughly a year. A realistic Bay Area new build from first meeting to move-in is often a couple of years, so plan for that horizon rather than a single season.

What should I do first before building on my lot?

Start with a feasibility and zoning review. Before you commit to a design you need to know the lot's zoning, allowable size and height, setbacks, and any site constraints such as slope, soils, drainage, or trees. That review tells you what you can actually build and prevents you from paying for drawings the city will not approve.

Do I need permits to build a new home in the Bay Area?

Permit requirements and timelines differ by city and county across the Bay Area, so exact fees and review durations are set by your local building department, not a fixed statewide number. Expect a plan check where the city reviews your drawings for code compliance, one or more rounds of corrections, and required inspections during construction. A design-build team that knows local processes can prepare a complete submittal to reduce back-and-forth.

Is design-build a good way to build a new home?

Often yes, for a new custom home. A design-build firm carries feasibility, architecture, engineering, permitting, and construction under one team and one contract, so there is a single point of accountability from the first site review to the final inspection. That removes the gap between the people who design the home and the crew that builds it, which keeps the budget and schedule realistic.

Key Takeaways

  • Building on your lot follows five steps in order: feasibility and zoning, design and engineering, permits and plan check, construction, and final inspection and move-in.
  • Start with feasibility - zoning, setbacks, and site conditions decide what you can build before any design money is spent.
  • Permit fees and timelines are set by your specific Bay Area city or county, so confirm the process for your address rather than assuming a fixed number.
  • A full new build often spans a couple of years, and a design-build team keeps design, permits, and construction under one contract and one point of accountability.
