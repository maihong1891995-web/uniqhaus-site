What's Included in a Whole House Remodel

When homeowners ask what is included in a whole house remodel, the honest answer is that it depends on how far you take it. A full home remodel is not a single fixed package - it is a set of choices about which rooms, finishes, and systems you touch. At the core, almost every whole-home project includes the kitchen, the bathrooms, new flooring throughout, fresh interior paint and finishes, and updated lighting and fixtures. From there, scope expands to the wiring, plumbing, and HVAC behind the walls and, on bigger projects, to walls, windows, and structure. This guide breaks down exactly what is included, level by level, so you know what a Bay Area quote should and should not cover. It pairs with our whole house remodel service and our Bay Area home remodel hub.

What a whole house remodel includes

A whole house remodel updates the interior of your home as a coordinated project rather than one room at a time. The most common scope includes the kitchen and bathrooms, flooring, paint and finishes, and interior lighting, with systems and structure added based on the home's condition and your goals. The table below shows each major area and whether it is typically part of a full home remodel.

AreaUsually included?What it covers
KitchenYesCabinets, countertops, backsplash, sink and faucet, and often the layout, on most whole-home projects
BathroomsYesVanities, tile, tub and shower, toilets, and fixtures across all bathrooms in the home
FlooringYesNew flooring throughout, commonly engineered wood, tile, or luxury vinyl for a consistent look
Paint and finishesYesInterior paint, trim, doors, baseboards, and hardware for a finished, cohesive interior
Electrical, plumbing, HVACOftenUpdated wiring, pipes, and ductwork where walls are already open, plus panel or system upgrades if needed
Windows and doorsSometimesNew or replacement windows and exterior doors when energy, code, or aesthetics call for it
StructureSometimesRemoving or moving walls, opening up floor plans, and beams or framing that require engineering and permits
ExteriorOptionalSiding, roofing, stucco, or curb-appeal work when added to the scope, not automatic on an interior remodel

The first four rows are the backbone of nearly every full home remodel. The bottom four are where scopes diverge and where two quotes for the "same" project can differ by six figures. Knowing which of these you actually need is the point of the design phase covered below.

What is included by scope level

The cleanest way to understand what is included is to think in three scope levels. Each one draws a different line on how deep the work goes, and that line decides what a contractor prices.

Cosmetic refresh. This is the lightest whole-home scope. It updates finishes only: new paint throughout, new flooring, refreshed or refaced cabinets, new countertops and fixtures, and updated lighting. The wiring, plumbing, ductwork, and wall layout stay exactly where they are. A refresh makes a dated home look current without opening walls, so it is faster and far less expensive. It does not fix a bad layout or aging systems.

Mid-range remodel. The most common whole house remodel in the Bay Area. It includes everything in a refresh, plus new kitchen and bathroom layouts, some wall changes, and systems work where the walls are already open. If a kitchen is gutted to the studs, new wiring, plumbing rough-ins, and a fresh vent hood run go in while access is easy. This level often includes select window replacements and minor structural changes, such as widening a doorway or removing one non-load-bearing wall.

Full gut renovation. The home is taken down to the studs, and sometimes the framing itself. This scope includes full replacement of electrical, plumbing, and HVAC across the house, new insulation and drywall, new windows and doors, and significant structural work such as opening up the entire floor plan or reconfiguring rooms. Everything from the studs out is new. This is the right level for older Bay Area homes with knob-and-tube wiring, galvanized pipes, or a layout that no longer works. What is usually not included

Just as important as what is included is what is not. Several items homeowners assume are part of a remodel are usually separate, and misreading this is a common source of budget surprises.

  • Furniture and decor. A remodel delivers finished, empty rooms. Sofas, beds, rugs, art, and window treatments are not part of a construction contract unless you hire a separate interior styling package.
  • Landscaping and hardscape. Yards, patios, decks, driveways, and planting are outdoor scopes. They are only included when you specifically add them to the project.
  • Appliances beyond the allowance. Most contracts carry an appliance allowance, a set dollar amount. If you choose a professional range or panel-ready refrigerator above that number, the difference is yours. The same applies to plumbing fixtures and tile allowances.
  • Detached structures and big-ticket add-ons. An ADU, garage conversion, pool, or solar array is its own project with its own permits, not a line inside an interior remodel.
  • Undisclosed conditions. Hidden issues found once walls open, such as old dry rot, failed wiring, or foundation problems, are handled as change orders rather than assumed in the base scope.

The rule is simple: if it is not written into the scope of work, it is not included. That is why a detailed, line-by-line scope with clear allowances matters far more than the headline price. A vague quote almost always leaves out work that returns later as a change order.

How scope drives cost and timeline

Because scope decides what is included, it also decides what you pay and how long you live in a construction zone. In the Bay Area's 2026 market, the ranges below give a realistic sense of how the three levels compare. These are planning figures, not quotes, since every home and finish level is different.

Scope levelTypical Bay Area costTypical timeline
Cosmetic refreshLow-to-mid five figures for a whole home, depending on size and finishesA few weeks to about two months
Mid-range remodelRoughly 200 to 400 dollars per square footAbout three to six months
Full gut renovationOften 500 dollars per square foot and upAbout six to twelve months

The pattern is clear: the deeper you go into systems and structure, the more the cost and calendar grow. Systems work is expensive because it is labor-intensive and hidden, and structural changes add engineering, permits, and inspections. Adding load-bearing wall removals, a new electrical panel, or a full HVAC replacement can each move the number meaningfully. For a detailed local breakdown, see our whole-house remodel cost guide for San Jose. The takeaway when comparing quotes is to compare scope first: a lower price usually means a lighter scope, not a better deal.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is included in a whole house remodel?

A whole house remodel usually includes the kitchen, bathrooms, flooring throughout, interior paint and finishes, and lighting and fixtures. Most full home remodels also update electrical, plumbing, and HVAC where the walls are open. Windows, doors, structural changes, and exterior work are included when the scope calls for them but are not automatic. The exact list depends on the scope level you choose.

Does a whole house remodel include new plumbing and electrical?

Not always. A cosmetic refresh keeps the existing wiring, pipes, and ducts and only updates finishes, so systems are not touched. A mid-range remodel usually upgrades systems where walls are already open, such as new wiring in a gutted kitchen. A full gut renovation replaces electrical, plumbing, and HVAC across the house. What is included depends on how far the walls come down.

What is not included in a whole house remodel?

Furniture, decor, landscaping, and appliance purchases beyond a stated allowance are usually not included in a whole house remodel contract. Detached structures, pools, and solar are separate projects unless you add them. Anything not written into the scope of work is not included, which is why a detailed, line-by-line scope matters more than the headline price.

How does scope affect whole house remodel cost and timeline?

Scope is the single biggest driver of both. A cosmetic refresh of a Bay Area home often runs in the low-to-mid five figures over a few weeks, a mid-range whole house remodel commonly lands around 200 to 400 dollars per square foot over three to six months, and a full gut renovation can exceed 500 dollars per square foot and run six to twelve months. Adding structural or systems work raises both cost and timeline.

How do I decide what to include in my remodel?

Start with a design phase that documents exactly what is and is not included before any demolition. At UniqHaus we define the scope room by room, set realistic allowances for finishes, flag structural and systems work early, and put it all in one written scope so there are no surprise change orders. That way the contract matches the home you actually want.

Key Takeaways

  • Nearly every whole house remodel includes the kitchen, bathrooms, flooring, and interior paint and finishes.
  • Electrical, plumbing, HVAC, windows, and structure are included based on scope level, not automatically.
  • Furniture, landscaping, and appliances above the allowance are usually not included in the contract.
  • Scope drives cost and timeline, so define exactly what is included in the design phase before demolition.
