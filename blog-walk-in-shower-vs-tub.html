The tub-versus-shower decision is the single biggest fixture choice in any bathroom remodel, and it drives both your budget and your resale story. In 2026, most Bay Area homeowners are converting a cramped tub-shower combo to a curbless walk-in shower - and that conversion typically adds $3,000-$8,000 over a like-for-like tub swap. This guide compares the two head to head on cost, resale, and aging in place, then gives you the one rule that keeps a shower conversion from hurting your home's value. It is a companion to our main San Jose bathroom remodel guide, which covers full cost tiers, permits, and the 8-14 week timeline.

Walk-in shower vs tub: which should you choose?

For a master or guest bath, choose a walk-in shower; for the home's only full bath or a kids' bath, keep a tub - and if you can only afford one change, a curbless walk-in adds about $3K-$8K but returns the most in daily use and buyer appeal. The decision comes down to who uses the room and whether the house still has a tub somewhere else. A primary suite is almost always better as a large walk-in shower; a second or third bathroom is where a tub earns its keep.

Three questions settle it fast. Is this the only bathtub in the house? If yes, keep a tub. Is this a master or ensuite that adults use daily? If yes, a walk-in shower is the upgrade almost everyone actually wants. Are you planning to stay in the home as you age? If yes, a curbless walk-in is the safest choice you can make. Everything below quantifies those trade-offs, and our bathroom remodeling service walks you through the same decision in person.

Cost comparison: walk-in shower vs tub

A like-for-like tub replacement is the cheaper option, while a curbless walk-in shower adds roughly $3,000-$8,000 on top - mostly for glass, waterproofing, and more tile. The gap comes from where the money goes: a shower is a fully tiled, waterproofed enclosure with a frameless glass panel, where a basic tub swap reuses much of the existing surround. The table below shows how the two compare on a mid-range Bay Area bathroom in 2026.

OptionWhat's involvedRelative 2026 cost
Like-for-like tub swapNew tub and surround in the same spot, same plumbing, minimal new tileBaseline (lowest)
Tub-shower combo refreshNew tub plus tiled walls and a shower valve, existing footprintBaseline + tile & valve
Curbless walk-in showerFull waterproofing, floor-to-ceiling tile, frameless glass, linear drain, often a bench+$3,000-$8,000 over a tub swap

Two things swing that number. First, whether you move the drain: keeping the shower drain and valve where the tub's plumbing already sits controls the cost, while relocating them can add $3,000-$8,000 of its own on top - the same layout rule that governs every fixture in a bathroom. Second, tile choice: porcelain runs $3-$10 per square foot in materials and natural stone $10-$50+, with labor to set it at $10-$25 per square foot, and a walk-in shower simply has more tiled surface than a tub surround. Resale and aging-in-place: what buyers want

In a master or guest bath, a walk-in shower usually adds more resale appeal than a tub - buyers expect an updated, spacious shower - and a mid-range bathroom remodel recoups about 66-74% of its cost nationally, typically more in high-value Bay Area markets. Updated bathrooms are a baseline expectation at Santa Clara County price points, and a dated tub-shower combo is one of the first things buyers mentally deduct. A clean walk-in with frameless glass and large-format tile is exactly the look that photographs well and holds its value.

Aging in place is where the walk-in truly separates from the tub. A curbless shower is the single best aging-in-place upgrade in a bathroom: there is no lip to step over, it accommodates a bench and grab bars, and it is wheelchair accessible - dramatically safer than climbing in and out of a tub. For homeowners planning to stay in a San Jose home for the long term, that safety is worth more than any resale percentage. The same refresh-don't-over-build logic that governs the whole room applies here: a well-designed walk-in beats an expensive luxury tub on both fronts, a pattern we cover across our home remodeling service.

When to keep a tub (and how many)

Keep at least one tub in the home - not one per bathroom, just one - because a house with zero bathtubs loses the family-buyer segment at resale. Parents with young children screen out listings that have no tub at all, so removing your last bathtub can quietly shrink your buyer pool. The fix is simple: one well-placed tub in a hall or kids' bath satisfies the family-resale rule, which frees you to make the master a full walk-in shower with a clear conscience.

A tub also stays the smarter choice in a few specific cases: when it is the home's only bathtub, in a kids' or hall bath where bathing small children matters, and when a homeowner genuinely soaks on a regular basis. Tubs are also the cheaper like-for-like replacement, and in a very compact bathroom a good tub-shower combo can beat forcing a small, tight walk-in that never feels generous. Design tips for a curbless walk-in shower

The design that makes a curbless walk-in feel bigger than its footprint comes down to four moves - continuous tile, a linear drain, frameless glass, and a bench - none of which add more than a modest share of the 15-20% tile line of the budget. A curbless entry with a linear drive means the same large-format tile runs from the bathroom floor straight into the shower, so the eye reads one continuous surface instead of a boxed-off stall. That visual trick is the whole reason walk-ins look so much larger than the tub they replaced.

A few specifics that pay off in a Bay Area bath: use large-format porcelain to minimize grout lines and make the room feel calm and larger; keep a single tiled feature wall rather than full-height stone everywhere to control the tile budget; specify a frameless glass panel or a fixed screen rather than a swinging door to open up sightlines; and plan the waterproofing and slope carefully, since a curbless floor has to drain without a curb to contain it. That last point is why waterproofing and tile take 1-2 weeks with cure time between coats - the part of the schedule you cannot rush. Frequently Asked Questions

Is a walk-in shower more expensive than a tub?

A curbless walk-in shower typically adds about $3,000-$8,000 over a like-for-like tub swap in the Bay Area, mostly for glass, tile, and waterproofing. A basic tub-and-surround replacement is the cheaper option, but a walk-in reads as more spacious and ages in place better.

Does a walk-in shower or a tub add more resale value?

For a master or guest bath, a walk-in shower usually adds more resale appeal because buyers expect an updated, spacious shower. The exception is family homes: keep at least one tub in the house, because buyers with young children screen out listings with no bathtub at all.

Is a walk-in shower better for aging in place?

Yes. A curbless walk-in shower is the single best aging-in-place upgrade in a bathroom because there is no lip to step over, it fits a bench and grab bars, and it is wheelchair accessible. It is far safer than climbing in and out of a tub.

How many tubs should a house have for resale?

Keep at least one tub in the home. You do not need a tub in every bathroom - a master or guest bath rarely needs one - but a house with zero bathtubs loses the family buyer segment at resale. One well-placed tub in a hall or kids' bath covers it.

Do I need a permit to convert a tub to a walk-in shower in San Jose?

Converting a tub to a walk-in shower usually needs a permit because it involves waterproofing, a new shower valve, and often moving the drain. San Jose bathroom permit fees typically run $400-$1,500, filed at SJPermits.org, and 2025 California water-efficiency rules apply to the new showerhead.

When is a bathtub still the better choice?

A tub still wins when it is the home's only bathtub, in a kids' or hall bath, or when a homeowner genuinely soaks regularly. Tubs are also the cheaper like-for-like replacement. In a compact bath, a good tub-shower combo can be smarter than forcing a small, tight walk-in.

Key Takeaways

  • A curbless walk-in shower adds about $3K-$8K over a like-for-like tub swap - the extra goes to glass, waterproofing, and more tile, not the fixture itself.
  • For a master or guest bath, a walk-in wins on resale and is the single best aging-in-place upgrade; a mid-range bathroom recoups about 66-74% of its cost nationally, typically more in the Bay Area.
  • Keep at least one tub in the home - a house with zero bathtubs loses family buyers - but you do not need a tub in every bathroom.
  • Keeping the drain and valve where the tub's plumbing already sits controls cost; relocating them can add $3K-$8K on its own.
