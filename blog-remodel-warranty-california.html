A contractor warranty on a California remodel is really three layers of protection, not one: the written workmanship warranty in your contract, the manufacturer warranties on the products installed in your home, and the time limits California law sets for construction defect claims. For Bay Area homeowners comparing bids on a whole-house remodel or a custom home, knowing which layer covers what is the difference between a quick fix and a long argument.

This guide explains each layer, what a strong warranty document looks like, and how to compare warranties side by side before you sign. It is general information, not legal advice. If you are already in a dispute, talk to a California construction attorney.

What warranty protection do you actually have on a California remodel?

You have up to three separate layers, and each one answers a different question. The contract warranty answers "will my builder come back and fix it?" The manufacturer warranty answers "who pays if the product itself fails?" California law answers "how long can I still hold someone responsible?"

  1. The written workmanship warranty. This is the promise in your contract that the contractor will repair defects in their own work for a set period. It is the layer you use most, because it is a phone call, not a lawsuit. Its length and scope are whatever the contract says, so it varies a lot between bids. Many contractors offer about one year.
  2. Manufacturer warranties. Windows, roofing, appliances, water heaters, flooring, and fixtures usually come with their own warranties from the maker. These cover product defects, not installation. Terms vary widely by brand and product line, and some require you to register the product or use an approved installer.
  3. California law. Even after a written warranty ends, state law gives homeowners a window to bring claims for construction defects, and the Contractors State License Board (CSLB) can act against a licensed contractor for a period of years. This layer is your backstop, not your first call.

The practical takeaway: the contract warranty is the one you control at signing. The other two exist either way, but they are slower and harder to use.

How do the warranty layers compare?

The written workmanship warranty is usually the shortest layer, while California's latent defect limit runs up to 10 years. Here is how they line up.

Notice that only the first three rows are promises someone makes to you in writing. The rest are rules that set outer limits. A good contract warranty means you rarely need to think about those outer limits at all.

How long is a contractor liable for defects in California?

In California, a homeowner generally has 4 years after substantial completion to sue over obvious defects and up to 10 years for hidden ones. Those are the outer limits, and they work differently from a warranty.

Patent defects: 4 years

Code of Civil Procedure section 337.1 sets a 4-year limit, measured from substantial completion, for claims about patent deficiencies. A patent defect is one a reasonable inspection would reveal: a crooked door frame, a visibly uneven floor, tile set out of level. Because you can see these, the law expects you to raise them sooner.

Latent defects: 10 years

Under section 337.15, claims for latent deficiencies, meaning defects not apparent by reasonable inspection, must be brought within 10 years of substantial completion. Think of a framing connection behind finished drywall or a concealed waterproofing failure that shows up years later. The 10-year clock runs from completion, not from the day you discover the problem, and the statute carves out cases of willful misconduct or fraudulent concealment.

What the CSLB can and cannot do

The CSLB states it has jurisdiction over a licensed contractor for up to 4 years from the act for patent defects and up to 10 years for latent structural defects. It also notes that a claim on an express written warranty can be brought within the duration of that warranty. A complaint can lead to discipline, including action against the contractor's license.

What the CSLB does not do is get your money back. Its CSLB complaint process page says the purpose of an investigation is not restitution and points homeowners to small claims court for limited damages, or an attorney for larger ones.

The contractor bond

Every active California license requires a $25,000 contractor bond, filed for the benefit of consumers damaged by defective construction and workers owed wages. It is real protection, but it is capped and can be claimed by more than one party. On a $300,000 remodel, $25,000 will not cover a serious failure. That is why the bond is a floor, not a plan.

These rules are summarized for general understanding. Deadlines can depend on the facts, the contract, and how a court reads them, so speak with a California construction attorney before relying on any specific date.

What should a good remodel warranty document include?

A good warranty is a written page you can hand to someone else and they would know exactly what is covered, for how long, and how to ask for a repair. If you cannot answer those three questions from the document, it is not finished.

  • It is in writing and part of the contract. A warranty mentioned in a sales meeting but missing from the signed contract is very hard to enforce.
  • A clear start date. Coverage should start at completion, ideally tied to a named event such as final inspection or a signed completion notice, not the day work began.
  • Separate terms for cosmetic and structural work. A drywall crack and a framing issue are different problems. Good warranties name each category and give each its own length.
  • A defined claim process. Who you contact, how (email, portal, phone), and what to include, such as photos and a short description.
  • A stated response time. Look for a commitment to acknowledge and schedule an inspection within a set number of days, with an emergency path for active leaks.
  • Clear exclusions. Common and reasonable exclusions include normal wear and tear, owner-supplied materials, damage from lack of maintenance, misuse, and work later altered by someone else. Exclusions are fine. Vague exclusions are not.
  • How manufacturer warranties are handled. The contractor should hand over product manuals, warranty registrations, and model numbers at closeout, and say whether they will help coordinate a manufacturer claim.
  • Transferability. If you might sell within a few years, ask whether the warranty transfers to the next owner. Some do, many do not. Either answer is fine if it is written down.

How do you compare warranties between contractor bids?

Put the warranty terms from each bid side by side, the same way you compare price. Two bids $15,000 apart can look very different once you price in what each one will fix for free after move-in.

Ask every contractor the same five questions and write down the answers:

  1. How long is your workmanship warranty, and is structural work covered separately?
  2. When does the clock start?
  3. Exactly what is excluded?
  4. How do I file a claim, and how fast do you respond?
  5. Is the warranty in the contract I will sign?

Then weigh who stands behind the work. A warranty is only as good as the company that will still be answering the phone in year two or year ten. Licensing, a real office, and a track record matter here, and our guide to choosing a Bay Area contractor walks through how to verify all of that on the CSLB site.

Red flags

  • A verbal-only warranty. "We always take care of our clients" is not a warranty. If it is not in the contract, assume it does not exist.
  • An unlicensed contractor offering a warranty. California requires a license for jobs of $500 or more in labor and materials. An unlicensed builder has no license for the CSLB to act on and no license bond behind the work, so their warranty has little backing.
  • No start date or no claim process. A warranty with no mechanics is a sentence, not a commitment.
  • Blanket exclusions. Language like "any condition not caused by contractor negligence" can quietly exclude almost everything.
  • Split responsibility. When a designer and a separate builder each warrant "their part," a defect at the seam between design and construction can leave you in the middle.

How do you keep your warranty coverage intact?

Most warranties exclude damage caused by missing maintenance, so a few simple habits protect both your home and your coverage.

  • Keep the closeout file. Save the contract, warranty page, permit final, product manuals, model numbers, and paint and finish schedules in one folder.
  • Register products right away. Some manufacturer warranties require registration within a set time after installation.
  • Report issues early, in writing. Send photos and a short email as soon as you notice a crack, sticking door, or stain. Early reports are easier to fix and create a clear record inside the warranty period.
  • Handle routine upkeep. Re-caulk wet areas, clean gutters before the rainy season, keep drainage pointed away from the foundation, and change HVAC filters on schedule.
  • Ask before you alter. Having another trade modify warrantied work, such as moving an outlet or cutting into a wall, can void coverage for that area. A quick call first avoids that.
  • Walk the house before the warranty ends. Schedule your own walkthrough a month or two before the cosmetic term expires and list anything you want reviewed.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long is a contractor liable for defects in California?

Generally 4 years after substantial completion for defects a reasonable inspection would reveal (CCP 337.1) and up to 10 years for hidden, latent defects (CCP 337.15). These are outer legal limits, separate from your contract warranty. This is general information, not legal advice.

Is a one-year warranty on a remodel enough?

Many contractors offer about one year, which covers early issues but not much else. On a whole-house remodel or custom home, look for longer coverage on structural work and a cosmetic term that runs through more than one full season cycle. UniqHaus offers 2 years cosmetic and 10 years structural.

Does a home remodel warranty transfer if I sell my house?

Only if the contract says so. Some workmanship warranties transfer to the next owner and many do not. Manufacturer warranties have their own transfer rules, so check each product's terms and keep the paperwork for buyers.

What is not covered by a contractor workmanship warranty?

Common exclusions are normal wear and tear, owner-supplied materials, damage from lack of maintenance or misuse, and work later changed by someone else. Product failures usually fall under the manufacturer warranty instead. Ask each contractor for the exact exclusion list in writing.

Can the CSLB make my contractor pay for repairs?

The CSLB can investigate and discipline a licensed contractor, but its complaint page says its purpose is not restitution. To recover money, homeowners typically use small claims court or consult an attorney. The required $25,000 contractor bond may also help, but it is capped.

What should I do if my contractor will not honor the warranty?

Send a dated written request with photos that cites the warranty section in your contract, and keep copies. If that fails, you can file a CSLB complaint and consider small claims or an attorney for larger losses. For any dispute, a California construction attorney can advise on your specific deadlines.
