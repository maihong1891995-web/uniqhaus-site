Title 24 for Remodels and Additions: What California's Energy Code Requires in 2026

Title 24 only regulates the parts of your home you add or change. Under the 2025 Energy Code, effective for permits filed on or after January 1, 2026, alterations must meet rules for the components you replace, additions follow separate addition rules (stricter window limits above 700 sq ft), and solar is required for new homes and new detached ADUs, not for additions. Every permit set includes a CF1R energy report, and some items need third-party field verification.

Title 24 is the part of every remodel or addition permit in California that decides how energy efficient the new work has to be. If you are planning an addition or major remodel in San Jose or anywhere in Santa Clara County, the rules that apply to you come from the California Energy Commission's 2025 Building Energy Efficiency Standards, which apply to building permit applications submitted on or after January 1, 2026.

The short version: Title 24 does not ask you to bring your whole existing house up to new-home standards. It regulates the parts you add or change. A kitchen remodel that replaces windows, a 400 square foot bedroom addition, a new detached ADU, and a ground-up custom home each trigger a different set of rules. This guide explains which rules, what paperwork the city expects, and how energy choices show up in your design and budget.

What does Title 24 actually cover on a remodel?

For a typical home project, "Title 24" means Part 6 of the California Building Standards Code, the Energy Code. It sets minimum performance for the building envelope (insulation, windows, air sealing), heating and cooling, ducts, water heating, lighting, and, for new homes, solar. A second part, CALGreen (Part 11), covers green building measures such as water-efficient fixtures and construction waste. The 2025 edition of CALGreen also took effect January 1, 2026, and the state housing agency publishes the residential mandatory measures and checklists.

The Energy Code sorts every project into one of three buckets, and the bucket drives almost everything else:

  • Alterations. Changes to an existing home that do not add conditioned floor area, such as replacing windows, a new HVAC system, or a full interior remodel.
  • Additions. New conditioned floor area attached to the existing home, including an attached ADU.
  • Newly constructed buildings. A new house, or a new detached ADU, which the Energy Commission treats as a newly constructed single-family building.

Within each bucket the code has two layers. Mandatory measures always apply and can never be traded away. On top of those, you choose a compliance path: prescriptive (meet a fixed checklist) or performance (model the design in state-approved software and show it uses no more energy than the code's reference home). We cover how to choose between them below.

Two points surprise most homeowners. First, an addition does not require solar. The Energy Commission's ADU guidance states that because attached ADUs are additions, PV is not required, and the same logic applies to a bedroom or family room addition. Second, a new detached ADU does need PV, but the Commission's 2025 guidance says the panels can be added to an existing solar system rather than placed on the ADU roof itself. How do windows, insulation, and HVAC rules apply in practice?

The rules bite hardest on four components. Here is how each plays out on a Bay Area project.

Windows and glass doors

In Climate Zone 4, the prescriptive target for new windows is a maximum U-factor of 0.27 and a maximum solar heat gain coefficient of 0.23. For replacement windows in an alteration, the 2025 code allows up to 75 square feet of replacement glass at a less strict 0.40 U-factor and 0.35 SHGC. Every new window anywhere in California must still meet a mandatory weighted average U-factor of 0.40. In practice, a large wall of glass in a new great room is the single design choice most likely to push an addition from the simple prescriptive path into performance modeling.

Insulation and air sealing

New walls, roofs, and floors in an addition must meet the insulation levels for your climate zone. When you open existing walls during a remodel, the newly exposed cavities are also an opportunity, because insulating them while they are open costs far less than doing it later.

Heating and cooling

For additions, the 2025 code added explicit load calculation and maximum system capacity rules (Section 150.2(a)1E), so the HVAC equipment has to be sized to the actual heating and cooling load rather than oversized "to be safe." For alterations, all new air-source heat pumps must receive field verification of minimum airflow and refrigerant charge. Extending an existing system to serve new rooms raises a practical question your designer should answer early: can the existing equipment and ducts handle the added load, or does the addition need its own system, such as a ductless heat pump?

Water heating

For new homes and detached ADUs, the 2025 prescriptive baseline is a heat pump water heater or solar water heating in every climate zone. For additions, the code now removes gas and electric tankless heaters from the prescriptive options when you add a water heater, and allows all-electric point-of-use water heaters in additions up to 500 square feet. Gas equipment can still be used in some cases through the performance path. If you install a heat pump water heater, new mandatory rules cover its air volume and ventilation, which affects where it can go in the house.

Prescriptive or performance: which path fits your project?

Most simple alterations use the prescriptive path, and most additions and new homes with real design ambition use the performance path. The trade-off is flexibility versus simplicity.

  • Prescriptive is a checklist. Each component must meet its listed value. It is quick and clear, but you cannot trade a better wall for more glass. It works well for a window replacement or a straightforward small addition.
  • Performance uses Energy Commission approved software (CBECC-Res 2025 for single-family homes) to model the whole design. You can exceed the code in one area to earn flexibility in another, for example better attic insulation and a heat pump water heater to support a larger window wall.

For an addition, the performance model can also include the existing house. That often helps: upgrading something in the existing home, such as replacing an old furnace with a heat pump, can offset a design choice in the addition you care about. This is where one team for design, energy, and construction pays off.

What paperwork does Title 24 add: CF1R, CF2R, and CF3R

Title 24 compliance runs on three forms, one for each stage of the project. When comparing bids, ask who prepares and pays for each.

  1. CF1R, Certificate of Compliance. Prepared during design and included in the permit set. It documents the compliance path and every energy feature the plan reviewer will check: insulation values, window specs, HVAC, water heating, and any field verification required.
  2. CF2R, Certificate of Installation. Completed by the installing contractors during construction to confirm that what was built matches the CF1R.
  3. CF3R, Certificate of Verification. Completed by a third-party rater who tests items such as duct leakage, HVAC airflow and refrigerant charge, or insulation quality, when the CF1R calls for it.

One change for 2026: field verification is now run through the Energy Commission's Energy Code Compliance (ECC) program, which replaced the former HERS program on January 1, 2026. HERS raters are now called ECC-Raters, and they register verification results with a state-approved ECC-Provider. Final inspection typically will not pass until the required forms are registered, so verification has to be scheduled into the construction plan, not left to the last week.

In San Jose, the CF1R is uploaded with the rest of the permit set through SJPermits.org. For the full sequence of plan check, corrections, and inspections, see our guide to the San Jose building permit process.

Do San Jose's local energy rules add anything?

For most additions and remodels, the state Energy Code is the main energy rulebook. San Jose's reach code was written for new construction: the city states that its all-electric and reach code requirements do not apply to additions or alterations, that attached ADUs and junior ADUs count as additions or alterations, and that newly built detached ADUs are covered as new buildings. Local gas rules across the Bay Area have shifted since a 2023 federal court decision on Berkeley's ordinance, so confirm the current status with the city or your designer before you finalize equipment choices for a new home or detached ADU.

How does Title 24 affect the cost of a remodel or addition?

Title 24 costs show up in two places: professional work (energy calculations and field verification) and the specification of the building itself. Most of the cost is decided in design.

  • Glass area and window quality. More and larger windows require higher-performance glazing or offsetting upgrades elsewhere.
  • HVAC strategy. Extending an existing system, replacing it, or adding a separate system for the addition each carries a different cost and a different verification scope.
  • Water heating. Moving to a heat pump water heater may require a new electrical circuit, space, and ventilation, and sometimes a panel upgrade.
  • Electrical capacity. All-electric equipment and, for new homes, battery-ready requirements can make a service or panel upgrade part of the scope.
  • Solar. Required for a new detached ADU or new home; not required for an addition.
  • Re-design after plan check. The most avoidable cost. A permit set that is redrawn because the energy report and the drawings do not match costs time as well as money.

For context on how energy rules sit inside total build cost, our California cost per square foot guide answers whether Title 24 adds to build cost. For a typical addition, UniqHaus sees total budgets in the $100,000 to $500,000+ range depending on size and finish, and we recommend a 10 to 20 percent contingency on remodel work. Energy compliance items are priced inside that itemized budget, not added later.

How a design-build team handles Title 24 inside the permit set

The energy report should be part of design, not a form filled in after the drawings are done. At UniqHaus, architecture, permit-ready drawings, and city coordination are handled in-house alongside construction, so the window schedule, HVAC plan, and CF1R are developed together and checked against the same itemized budget. Once you approve that budget, the price is fixed; changes you request after approval are priced as change orders.

Two of our projects show the range of additions this applies to. At Blair in Sunnyvale, a full-home remodel and addition took a 3 bed, 1 bath house of about 1,518 sq ft to a 4 bed, 3.5 bath home of about 1,918 sq ft, with every room rebuilt from the studs. That is roughly a 400 sq ft addition, which under today's rules would fall in the 700 sq ft or less addition category. At Bird Avenue in San Jose, a major addition and whole-home remodel grew the home from about 1,046 to about 2,207 sq ft, with 4 bedrooms, 3 baths, a new porch, rear patio, and attached garage. An addition of that size, more than 1,100 sq ft, would sit in the larger addition category with the full new-home window table. Both projects were permitted under earlier code cycles, so the categories here illustrate how the 2025 rules would sort them today.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need solar panels for a home addition in California?

No. Under the 2025 Energy Code, solar PV is required for newly constructed homes, including new detached ADUs, but not for additions. The Energy Commission's 2025 ADU guidance confirms that attached ADUs are additions, so PV is not required for them either.

Does replacing windows require Title 24 compliance?

Yes. Replacement windows are an alteration and must meet the 2025 Energy Code. In Climate Zone 4, which includes San Jose, the prescriptive limit is a 0.27 U-factor and 0.23 SHGC, with an exception that lets up to 75 sq ft of replacement glass meet a 0.40 U-factor and 0.35 SHGC.

What is a CF1R and who prepares it?

The CF1R is the Certificate of Compliance, the energy report that goes into your permit set. It is usually prepared by an energy consultant working with your designer, and it lists every energy feature the plan reviewer and inspector will check.

Is HERS testing still required in 2026?

The testing is still required where the energy report calls for it, but the program has a new name. On January 1, 2026, HERS field verification moved into the Energy Commission's Energy Code Compliance (ECC) program, and HERS raters are now ECC-Raters.

Does a new detached ADU have to be all-electric in San Jose?

The state Energy Code uses heat pumps as the baseline for new detached ADUs, and San Jose's reach code treats newly built detached ADUs as new buildings. Local gas rules have changed since a 2023 court decision, so confirm current requirements with the city before choosing equipment.

Which Title 24 code applies if my permit was filed in 2025?

The code cycle is set by the date your building permit application is submitted. Applications submitted on or after January 1, 2026 use the 2025 Energy Code; earlier applications generally stay under the 2022 code. Ask your designer which forms and software version your project uses.

Key Takeaways

  • The 2025 Energy Code applies to permit applications submitted on or after January 1, 2026.
  • Additions do not require solar; new homes and new detached ADUs generally do.
  • Additions over 700 sq ft must meet the full new-home window table (0.27 U-factor and 0.23 SHGC in Climate Zone 4).
  • Every permit set needs a CF1R; required field tests are now done by ECC-Raters, formerly HERS raters.
