What to Expect at a Remodel Consultation (and How to Prepare)

A remodel consultation is a conversation to test feasibility, budget fit, and next steps, and it takes as long as your project needs. It is not a final bid. Bring your address and lot info, photos of problem areas, a ranked wish list, a budget range plus a 10 to 20 percent contingency, and any known issues. You should leave with a scope direction, a rough budget band, a timeline expectation, and one clear next step, such as a design package.

A remodel consultation is a working conversation, not a sales pitch and not a final bid. At UniqHaus there is no fixed time limit; it runs as long as your project and your questions need. If you are a San Jose or South Bay homeowner about to sit down with a design-build firm for the first time, knowing what to expect at a remodel consultation, and what to bring, is the difference between a vague chat and a meeting that gives you a real direction, a realistic budget band, and a clear next step.

This guide is the prep sheet we wish every homeowner had before that first meeting. It covers what the meeting is for, a checklist of what to bring, how to think about money before you walk in, the questions you will be asked, what you should leave with, and the signals that tell you whether a firm is worth a second meeting. If you are earlier than this and still sorting out the order of a big project, start with where to start a whole house remodel and come back when you are ready to talk to someone.

What is a remodel consultation actually for?

A consultation exists to answer three questions: is your project feasible, roughly what range will it cost, and what is the next step to get a real number. That is all it can honestly do in one sitting, and that is enough.

Here is what a good first meeting covers:

  • Goals. Why you are remodeling now, what is not working in the house, and what success looks like in two years.
  • Scope direction. Whether your wish list points to a focused kitchen and bath refresh, a whole-house remodel, an addition, an ADU, or a rebuild.
  • Constraints. Lot size, the age and condition of the house, known structural or system issues, HOA rules, and anything the city is likely to care about.
  • Budget fit. Whether your number and your scope are in the same neighborhood, and what to trim or phase if they are not.
  • Process. How the firm takes you from this conversation to drawings, permits, pricing, and construction.

Here is what it is not: a final bid. Nobody can responsibly give you a fixed price for a whole-house remodel or an addition from one conversation and a few photos. A real price depends on drawings, engineering, selected finishes, and what the city requires. If someone hands you a firm total at the end of a first meeting, treat it as a guess, not a commitment.

What should you bring to a contractor consultation?

Bring about nine things, and most of them you already have on your phone or in a drawer. The goal is to replace guesswork with facts so the conversation can move straight to your actual house.

Bring thisWhy it matters
Property address and basic lot info (lot size, year built, current square footage)Lot size and setbacks decide what you can add. Year built hints at what is behind the walls, from older wiring to an aging foundation.
Existing plans or as-builts, if you have themSaves time measuring and shows past changes. In San Jose, many permit records can be looked up online at SJPermits.org before you go.
Photos of problem areasCracks, stains, sagging floors, and tired systems tell the team more than a description can.
Wish list ranked into must-have and nice-to-haveRanking is what lets the firm protect your priorities when the budget gets tight.
Budget range, including the most you would spendA range lets the team match scope to money instead of designing something you cannot build.
Timeline constraintsA new baby, a lease ending, school schedules, or a move-out window all shape phasing and sequencing.
Inspiration images (5 to 15, not 200)A short, curated set shows your taste faster than words. Note what you like in each one.

If you are planning an ADU or addition, add one more item: a rough sketch of where you imagine it on the lot. It does not need to be accurate. It just gives the conversation a starting point.

How should you think about budget before the meeting?

Walk in with a range, not a single number, and check it against typical Bay Area costs first. These are the ranges we use as a starting point for planning:

  • Whole-house remodel: about $100 to $500 per square foot, with most mid-range projects at $200 to $400 per square foot.
  • Kitchen or bath remodel: about $30,000 to $150,000+.
  • Home addition: about $100,000 to $500,000+.
  • ADU: about $150,000 to $400,000.
  • Custom home: about $400 to $700+ per square foot.

Then do two quick checks. First, multiply. A 2,000 sq ft whole-house remodel at a mid-range $250 per square foot is about $500,000 before contingency. If your number is $200,000, you are not in the wrong place, but you are likely looking at a more focused scope or a phased plan, and it is better to know that in the first meeting. Second, set aside a contingency of 10 to 20 percent for what nobody can see until walls are open. Older homes sit at the higher end of that range.

Be honest about your number. Many homeowners lowball their budget in a first meeting to avoid being upsold. It usually backfires: the firm designs to the smaller number, and you end up redesigning later. A good firm uses your real ceiling to protect your must-haves, not to fill it. What questions will the firm ask you?

Expect the firm to ask more questions than you do in the first half of the meeting. That is a good sign. A design-build team needs to understand the house, the people living in it, and the money before it can say anything useful. Typical questions include:

  1. Why now? A growing family, working from home, a failing system, or finally having the budget. The trigger shapes the priorities.
  2. How long do you plan to stay? Five years versus twenty changes how much it makes sense to invest in systems, layout, and finishes.
  3. What does a normal weekday look like in this house? Where you cook, work, and gather tells a designer more than a room count.
  4. What must stay, and what can go? Walls, rooms, a fireplace, a tree, a view.
  5. What have you already done? Past permits, prior bids, an inspection report, or a design started with someone else.
  6. Can you live in the house during construction? This affects phasing, schedule, and cost.
  7. Who makes the decisions? If two people share the call, both should be in the room.

You will have your own questions too, about license, insurance, payments, and warranties. We have a separate list of questions to ask a contractor before hiring, so we will not repeat it here. Print it and bring it.

What should you walk away with after the first meeting?

You should leave with four things: a scope direction, a rough budget band, a timeline expectation, and one defined next step. If you are missing any of them, ask before you leave.

  • Scope direction. A plain-language read on what your project really is. For example: "This is a whole-house remodel with a modest rear addition, not a rebuild."
  • A rough budget band. A range tied to scope, such as "projects like this usually land between X and Y," with an explanation of what moves it up or down. Not a bid, but not a shrug either.
  • Timeline expectations. For context, a whole-house remodel typically runs about 6 to 12 months in total: roughly 2 to 3 months for design and permits, then 4 to 9 months of construction. A cosmetic refresh can be 3 to 4 months; a full gut can run 9 to 12 months or more.
  • A clear next step. Usually a paid design phase, with a stated price, deliverables, and turnaround, so you know exactly what you are buying before you buy it.

Write these down in the car before you forget them. If you are meeting more than one firm, these four items are how you compare them fairly.

What are the green flags and red flags in a first meeting?

The first meeting tells you how a firm will behave for the next 6 to 12 months. Pay attention to how they handle uncertainty, money, and your questions.

Green flags

  • They ask about your life and priorities before talking about finishes.
  • They give real number ranges and explain what drives them.
  • They say "we will not know until we see the drawings" when that is the honest answer.
  • They explain how pricing works after design, and how owner-requested changes are handled in writing.
  • They name who handles permits, and who your point of contact will be.
  • They share a license number you can verify in under a minute on the CSLB license lookup.

Red flags

  • A firm total for a major project after one conversation, with no drawings.
  • Pressure to sign the same day, or a "discount" that expires tonight.
  • A request for a large deposit up front. California generally limits a home improvement down payment to $1,000 or 10 percent of the contract price, whichever is less, as the Contractors State License Board explains.
  • Vague answers on permits, or a suggestion to skip them.
  • No interest in your known issues, or brushing off cracks and leaks you point out.
  • Promises that sound too clean: "no surprises ever," "never over budget."

None of these flags require expertise to spot. You only need to notice whether the answers are specific and calm, or vague and rushed.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does a remodel consultation take?

There is no fixed length. At UniqHaus the first consultation runs as long as your project needs: a focused kitchen refresh may need a short conversation, while a whole-house remodel or addition usually takes longer. That is enough time to cover goals, constraints, a budget range, and the next step, but not enough to produce a final price. Plan for both decision-makers to attend.

Is a remodeling consultation free?

Many design-build firms offer a free first consultation. At UniqHaus the consultation is free with no obligation; the paid step comes after, with a 3D design package at $1 per square foot that is credited when you build with us.

Should I tell a contractor my real budget?

Yes. A real range, including your ceiling, lets the firm design to what you can actually build and protect your must-haves. Hiding the number usually leads to a design you have to cut back later, which costs time and money.

Can a contractor give me a price at the first meeting?

A reliable firm can give a rough budget band based on scope and typical costs, such as $200 to $400 per square foot for a mid-range whole-house remodel. A fixed price needs drawings, finish selections, and permit requirements, so treat any firm total from a first meeting as an estimate.

What should I do after meeting with a remodeling contractor?

Write down the scope direction, budget band, timeline, and next step you were given, then verify the license on the CSLB website. If you are comparing firms, compare those four items side by side rather than the friendliness of the meeting.

Do I need plans before talking to a design-build firm?

No. A design-build firm creates the plans as part of its process. Existing plans or as-builts help if you have them, but photos, lot info, and a ranked wish list are enough to start.

Key Takeaways

  • A consultation tests feasibility, budget fit, and next steps; it is not a final bid.
  • Bring 9 things, led by lot info, photos of problem areas, a ranked wish list, and an honest budget range.
  • Add a 10 to 20 percent contingency to your budget before you walk in, especially for an older home.
  • Leave with 4 things: scope direction, a rough budget band, a timeline expectation, and one defined next step.
