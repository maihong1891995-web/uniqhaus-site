Townhouse Remodel HOA Approval: What You Can Change and How to Get It Approved

In most Bay Area townhouses, paint and like-for-like finishes need little or no HOA review, while layout changes, plumbing moves, wall removal, flooring changes, windows, and EV chargers usually need written HOA approval plus a city permit. California's Davis-Stirling Act requires your HOA to decide in good faith and in writing (Civil Code 4765), and EV charger requests not denied within 60 days are deemed approved. Plan on roughly one to two months for HOA review.

A townhouse remodel in the Bay Area usually needs two separate approvals: your HOA's architectural review for anything that touches shared structure, the exterior, or neighbors, and a city building permit for electrical, plumbing, mechanical, or structural work. Getting HOA approval first, in writing, is what keeps a San Jose townhouse kitchen or bath project from stalling halfway through.

Most townhouse owners we talk to are not worried about design. They are worried about the unknowns: what the board will say, how long it takes, and whether a contractor will cut corners in a building they share with neighbors. This guide walks through what typically needs approval, what California law requires of your HOA, and how to plan the work so the rules shape the design instead of derailing it.

Which rules apply to a townhouse remodel?

Three layers of rules apply to almost every townhouse: state law, your association's governing documents, and your city's building code. Knowing which layer controls which decision saves weeks.

  • State law. Most Bay Area townhouse communities are "common interest developments" under California's Davis-Stirling Act (Civil Code 4100). The Act sets the floor for how your HOA must handle remodel requests.
  • Your CC&Rs, bylaws, and architectural guidelines. These decide what needs approval in your community, what materials are allowed, and which parts of the building you own versus the association.
  • City permits. In San Jose, building permits are filed through SJPermits.org. The city reviews code and safety. It does not enforce your CC&Rs, and your HOA cannot waive city rules.

One detail matters more than people expect: how your townhouse is legally described. In some communities you own the unit's structure and the land under it, and the HOA controls the exterior look. In others, your townhouse is legally a condominium, and you own only the interior "airspace" while walls, roof, and pipes in the walls belong to the association. Your CC&Rs and the subdivision map spell this out. That one definition decides whether moving a wall is your call or theirs.

What does California law say about HOA approval?

California gives you the right to remodel inside your own unit, with limits. Under Civil Code 4760, subject to your governing documents and the law, an owner may make improvements within the boundaries of their separate interest as long as the work does not impair structural integrity or mechanical systems or lessen the support of common areas. Changes to the exterior appearance must follow the governing documents.

When your CC&Rs do require approval, Civil Code 4765 sets the rules the HOA must follow:

  • A fair, reasonable, and expeditious procedure. The process must be written into the governing documents and include a maximum time for the association to respond.
  • Good faith decisions. A decision cannot be unreasonable, arbitrary, or capricious.
  • Consistent with the law. The HOA cannot approve or deny in a way that violates building codes, fair housing law, or other laws.
  • Written decisions. A denial must explain why and describe how to request reconsideration.
  • Board reconsideration. If a committee denied your request, you can ask the board to reconsider at an open meeting. If the board itself made the decision at a properly held meeting, this step does not apply.
  • Annual notice. The association must tell members each year which changes need approval and share its review procedure.

Notice what the statute does not do: it does not set a single statewide deadline for every request. Your own governing documents set the response time. Read that number before you set a construction start date.

EV chargers are the exception with a fixed clock. Under Civil Code 4745, unreasonable restrictions on charging stations are void, and an application not denied in writing within 60 days is deemed approved, unless the delay comes from a reasonable request for more information. The owner pays for installation and electricity, and for a charger in a common area or exclusive use common area, the owner must carry liability coverage and give the HOA a certificate of insurance within 14 days of approval.

What can I change in my townhouse without HOA approval?

Paint, finishes, and like-for-like replacements inside your unit are usually the easiest approvals, and sometimes need none at all. What is usually off-limits in a townhouse?

Four categories are restricted in nearly every townhouse community, because they affect more than one home.

Party walls and structure

The wall you share with a neighbor is rarely yours to cut into. It often carries a fire rating and sound separation, and in many communities it is common area. Recessed outlets, niches, and speaker cutouts in a party wall are a frequent cause of denials. Interior walls inside your unit are often fair game, but bearing walls still need engineering and a permit.

Plumbing stacks and shared lines

Vertical drain stacks, vents, and main supply lines often serve more than one unit. Moving a sink a few feet along the same wall is usually workable. Moving a toilet to the other side of the bathroom may mean new drain routing through a slab or floor framing you do not own. This is the single biggest reason townhouse bath layouts stay close to the original.

The exterior envelope

Windows, doors, siding, roofs, and patio covers shape how the community looks. Expect the HOA to control color, style, and sometimes the manufacturer. A new window size usually also changes framing, which means both approval and a permit.

Noise between units

Hard flooring over a neighbor's ceiling is one of the most common sources of HOA disputes. If your CC&Rs set a sound rating, build to it and keep the product data sheet. It is much cheaper than tearing out a finished floor.

How long does HOA approval take, and what do you submit?

Plan for about one to two months from first submittal to written approval, depending on your HOA's meeting schedule and the response time in your governing documents. Many architectural committees meet monthly, so a missing document can cost you a full cycle.

A complete package usually includes:

  1. The HOA's application form, signed by the owner.
  2. A scope of work in plain language, room by room.
  3. Floor plans showing existing and proposed layouts, with any wall, plumbing, or electrical changes marked.
  4. Material and finish specs, including flooring underlayment data if hard surfaces are involved.
  5. Renderings or elevations for anything visible from outside.
  6. Your contractor's license number and certificate of insurance. You can check any license on the CSLB website.
  7. A proposed schedule, work hours, and plan for parking, deliveries, and debris.

A practical sequence that keeps things moving: finish design, submit to the HOA, then file for the city permit once you have written HOA approval or while it is pending if your HOA allows it. Ordering materials before both approvals are in hand is the most common way owners lose money on a townhouse project.

What does the HOA expect during construction?

Approval is only half the job. Most associations also set rules for how work happens on site, and breaking them can lead to fines or a stop-work notice from the board.

  • Work hours. Many HOAs limit noisy work to weekday daytime hours and restrict weekends.
  • Insurance certificates. Expect the HOA to require a certificate of insurance from your contractor, often naming the association as additional insured, before work starts.
  • Parking and staging. Guest spaces, fire lanes, and shared driveways usually cannot hold trucks or dumpsters. Ask where materials can be staged.
  • Elevators and common hallways. In stacked or elevator buildings, you may need to reserve the elevator and protect walls and floors.
  • Water and power shutoffs. If a shutoff affects neighbors, the HOA will want advance notice.
  • Deposits. Some associations collect a refundable deposit to cover damage to common areas.

Living next to an active jobsite is stressful for neighbors too. A contractor who follows the rules without being reminded protects your relationship with the people on the other side of the wall.

How much does it cost to remodel a townhouse?

Kitchen and bath remodels in the Bay Area typically run $30,000 to $150,000+, depending on size, layout changes, and finishes. Townhouse projects follow the same cost drivers, with a few HOA-specific ones on top: sound-rated underlayment, extra protection for shared spaces, tighter work hours, and design time spent fitting your plan around stacks and party walls. For detailed line items, see our kitchen remodel cost guide and bathroom remodel cost guide. UniqHaus projects start at $50,000, and we recommend keeping a 10-20 percent contingency.

The cheapest way to control cost in a townhouse is to design within the rules from day one. Keeping wet walls where they are, working with the existing window openings, and choosing compliant flooring up front avoids redesign fees and resubmittals later.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need HOA approval to remodel my kitchen?

It depends on your CC&Rs. A same-layout cabinet and counter update often needs little or no HOA review, while moving plumbing, removing walls, or changing windows almost always needs written approval. Check your architectural guidelines, which your HOA must share with members each year under Civil Code 4765.

Can my HOA deny my remodel?

Yes, but under California law the decision must be made in good faith, cannot be unreasonable, arbitrary, or capricious, and a denial must be in writing with an explanation. If a committee denied it, you can ask the board to reconsider at an open meeting.

Can I put hardwood or tile floors in my townhouse?

Usually yes, but many HOAs with attached or stacked units require sound-rated underlayment when you replace carpet with a hard surface. Get the required rating in writing from your HOA before you buy flooring.

How long does an HOA have to approve an EV charger in California?

Under Civil Code 4745, an EV charger application that is not denied in writing within 60 days is deemed approved, unless the delay comes from a reasonable request for more information. You will still need a city electrical permit.

Do I need a city permit if my HOA already approved my remodel?

Yes, if the work includes electrical, plumbing, mechanical, or structural changes. HOA approval and a city building permit are separate. In San Jose, permits are filed through SJPermits.org.

Who pays if my remodel damages a neighbor's unit or common area?

In most communities the owner who ordered the work is responsible, which is why HOAs ask for contractor insurance certificates and sometimes a refundable deposit. Read the indemnity and insurance sections of your CC&Rs before work starts.

Key Takeaways

  • Two gates: written HOA approval for shared structure, exterior, and noise; a city permit for electrical, plumbing, mechanical, or structural work.
  • Civil Code 4765 requires good faith, written decisions, and board reconsideration of committee denials at an open meeting.
  • EV charger applications not denied in writing within 60 days are deemed approved under Civil Code 4745.
  • Kitchen and bath remodels typically run $30,000-$150,000+; keep a 10-20 percent contingency and design around stacks and party walls.
