Guest House vs ADU in California: What Is the Real Difference?

An ADU is a legal second home defined by California law, with its own kitchen, bath, and sleeping space, and it can be rented (cities may require terms of 30 days or more). A guest house is not a state-law category. In San Jose, a backyard building without a kitchen is an accessory building limited to two plumbing fixtures and no sleeping quarters, so it cannot be rented as a home. If someone will live there or pay rent, build an ADU, which typically costs $150,000 to $400,000 in the Bay Area.

In California, the real difference in guest house vs ADU comes down to one word: kitchen. An ADU is a legal second home with its own kitchen, bath, and sleeping space, and you can rent it. A "guest house" is not a category in state law at all. In San Jose and most South Bay cities, a backyard building without a kitchen is regulated as an accessory building, and it usually cannot be rented or even slept in.

That matters if you are planning space for aging parents, an adult child, or rental income. Pick the wrong label at the design stage and you can end up with a building you cannot legally use the way you intended. This guide explains how each one is defined, how San Jose treats them, and how to choose.

What is the legal difference between a guest house and an ADU?

An ADU is defined by state law, and a guest house is not. California Government Code section 66313 defines an accessory dwelling unit as an attached or detached residential unit that provides "complete independent living facilities for one or more persons" on a lot with a primary home. In practice that means permanent provisions for living, sleeping, eating, cooking, and sanitation. The ADU rules now sit in Chapter 13 of the Government Code (sections 66310 to 66342), after California moved them out of the old section 65852.2 in 2024. The state housing department explains how cities must apply them in its ADU Handbook.

"Guest house" is a common name, not a legal one. When people say guest house, they usually mean one of three things:

  • A backyard room without a kitchen, such as a studio, home office, gym, or pool house. Cities regulate this as an accessory building or accessory structure.
  • A small unit with a kitchen. Legally, that is an ADU, whatever you call it, and it follows ADU rules.
  • A lodging business. Some zoning codes, including San Jose's, use "guesthouse" for a building that rents rooms to guests. That is a commercial-style use and not what most homeowners have in mind.

The takeaway: if the building has a kitchen and someone will live there, plan it as an ADU from day one. A JADU is a third option inside the main house, covered in our JADU vs ADU guide.

How does San Jose treat a backyard guest house?

San Jose is stricter than many homeowners expect. Under the city's zoning code (San Jose Municipal Code section 20.200.020), an accessory building is a separate, subordinate building whose use is "purely incidental" to the main house, and it may not contain living space or sleeping quarters. The city's accessory building handout adds practical limits: a maximum of two plumbing fixtures (for example, a toilet and a sink), no living or sleeping space, and no air conditioning. A building permit is needed once the structure is 120 square feet or larger.

So in San Jose, a detached "guest house" with a bed and a shower is not really an option as an accessory building. If you want a family member or tenant to sleep there, the legal path is usually an ADU. A kitchen-free backyard studio still works well as an office, gym, art room, or pool house.

Other South Bay cities such as Sunnyvale, Campbell, Los Gatos, and Saratoga write their own accessory building rules. Some allow a half bath, some cap height or size differently, and a few allow a sleeping room under specific conditions. Rules change, so confirm with your city's planning desk before you design. This is general information, not legal advice.

Can you rent a guest house in California?

Usually not, if it has no kitchen. A building that is not a dwelling unit is meant to serve the main house, so renting it to a separate household is generally not allowed. In San Jose, where accessory buildings may not have sleeping quarters, renting one as living space would conflict with the zoning definition. Your family can use it as an office or hobby room, but a tenant cannot live there.

An ADU is built to be rented. State law lets cities require that ADU rentals be for terms of 30 days or longer, which is why many Bay Area cities restrict short-term stays like Airbnb in ADUs. San Jose has its own short-term rental rules, and they treat ADUs differently from the primary home. Confirm the current rules with the city before you plan on nightly income.

If rent is a goal, the ADU is the only one of the two that gets you there. Our ADU rental income guide covers what Bay Area units earn and how long payback takes.

How do permits, property taxes, and value compare?

An ADU gets a protected, ministerial approval path, while a guest house goes through normal local review. Here is what that means for your project.

Permits

Both need a building permit in San Jose, filed through SJPermits.org. An ADU application must be approved or denied within 60 days once it is complete, without a public hearing. An accessory building has no state deadline, but it is usually a simpler set of drawings. Either way, the biggest time saver is a complete, accurate submittal.

Property taxes

Both trigger a new construction assessment, but only for what you add. The Santa Clara County Assessor's new construction booklet explains that new construction is added at market value while your existing home and land keep their prior assessed value. The tax increase follows the value of what you build, so a full ADU will usually add more to the tax bill than a simple studio.

Resale value

A permitted ADU is a second legal home on the lot. Buyers and appraisers can see its income potential, and under recent state law some ADUs can even be sold separately where a city opts in (see selling an ADU separately). A guest house adds pleasant square footage, but it is not a second unit. One caution for both: unpermitted work can create problems when you sell or refinance.

Which should you build?

Choose by use, not by name. A simple test: if anyone will sleep there regularly or pay you rent, plan an ADU.

When a guest house makes sense

  • You need a home office, studio, gym, or pool house, and nobody will live in it.
  • You want a smaller, simpler build without a kitchen, laundry, or separate utilities.
  • Overnight guests can stay in the main house, and the backyard room is for daytime use.
  • Your lot is tight and a small accessory building fits better than a full unit.

When an ADU is the better investment

  • A parent, adult child, or caregiver will live there full time and needs a kitchen and bath.
  • You want rental income now or later.
  • You want the strongest state protections on size, setbacks, and review time.
  • You care about long-term value and flexibility, since an ADU can shift from family use to rental and back.

Many multigenerational families land on the ADU because it covers both stories: parents now, a tenant later. If you are weighing adding space onto the main house instead, our ADU vs home addition comparison walks through that choice.

Can you convert a guest house into an ADU later?

Yes, and state law encourages it, but it is a real permit project, not a paperwork change. California lets homeowners convert an existing accessory structure into an ADU, and San Jose's ADU rules allow units within an existing accessory building. Expect these changes:

  • Kitchen. You add a cooking area, sink, and appliances, plus the electrical circuits and venting they need.
  • Plumbing and sewer. A full bath and kitchen exceed the two-fixture limit, so drain lines are often upsized or rerouted to the main sewer lateral.
  • Energy and comfort. A building that was unconditioned now needs heating, insulation, and windows that meet current energy code.
  • Fire and life safety. Smoke and carbon monoxide alarms, egress windows in the sleeping room, and fire-rated walls if the building is close to the property line or the house.
  • Utilities. Conversions often stay on the main house's meters. A larger electrical panel is common.
  • Permits. A new ADU permit with full drawings. The state ADU rules generally let an existing structure keep its current setbacks when converted.

If an ADU is even a possibility in five years, design the guest house so it can convert later. Plan the slab for future drain lines, size the electrical service with room to grow, and leave wall space for a kitchen. That costs little now and can save a lot of demolition later. Frequently Asked Questions

Can you live in a guest house in San Jose?

Not as a separate home if it is permitted as an accessory building. San Jose's zoning code says accessory buildings may not contain living space or sleeping quarters. To have someone live in a backyard unit, build or convert it as an ADU.

Is a guest house cheaper to build than an ADU?

Often, because it has no kitchen and fewer plumbing, mechanical, and utility needs. The final number depends on size, foundation, and finishes. A full Bay Area ADU typically costs $150,000 to $400,000; see our San Jose ADU cost guide.

Can I put a kitchen in my backyard guest house?

Adding a full kitchen usually turns the building into a dwelling unit, which means it must be permitted as an ADU. In San Jose, accessory buildings are capped at two plumbing fixtures, so a kitchen plus a bathroom will not fit that category.

Do I need a permit for a backyard guest house in California?

Yes in most cases. In San Jose, a building permit is required for an accessory building of 120 square feet or more. Other cities set their own thresholds, so check locally.

Can I Airbnb my ADU in San Jose?

State law lets cities require ADU rentals to be 30 days or longer, and San Jose regulates short-term rentals separately from long-term ones. Confirm the current rules with the city before you count on nightly income.

Does a guest house or ADU raise my property taxes?

Both can. The county assessor adds the market value of the new construction to your existing assessed value, while your house and land keep their prior base. A full ADU usually adds more value, so it usually adds more tax.

Key Takeaways

  • An ADU is defined in state law (Gov. Code 66313) and must have complete independent living facilities, including a kitchen; a guest house is not a state-law category.
  • In San Jose, accessory buildings allow at most 2 plumbing fixtures and no living or sleeping space, and need a permit at 120 sq ft or more.
  • Only an ADU can be rented as a home; cities may require ADU rentals of 30 days or more, and ADUs under 750 sq ft pay no impact fees.
  • If anyone will sleep there or pay rent, plan an ADU ($150,000 to $400,000 in the Bay Area); design a guest house to convert later if an ADU is likely.
