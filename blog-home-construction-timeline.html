The most common question at the start of any project is not "how much?" but "how long?" - and the honest answer is that a home construction timeline depends entirely on what you are building. A bathroom refresh and a ground-up custom home run on completely different clocks, yet both move through the same underlying sequence of phases. This guide maps the whole picture: realistic start-to-finish timelines by project type, the construction phases every job passes through, and the handful of things that actually cause delays in the Bay Area. It sits alongside our hub on design-build vs a general contractor and its sibling spoke on how long building permits take.

How long does home construction or a remodel take?

From design to done, a typical Bay Area project takes anywhere from about 8-14 weeks for a bathroom to 12-24 months for a custom home in 2026. Those numbers cover the entire journey - not just the days a crew is on site, but the design and permitting that come first. That distinction matters, because most homeowners picture only the construction and are surprised that planning and approvals often take as long as the physical work.

The length is driven by three things: the scope of the project, whether it adds square footage or moves structure (which triggers a longer permit review), and how well the design, permitting, and procurement stages overlap. A like-for-like remodel that keeps the existing footprint moves fastest; anything that adds space, changes the structure, or requires plan review takes longer. The table below sets realistic expectations by project type, and the section after it explains the phases every one of these projects shares.

A bathroom is the quickest because it is a small, self-contained space; a kitchen runs a little longer thanks to cabinetry lead times and more trades. A custom home sits at the far end, since it combines full architectural design, the longest plan-review path, and every construction phase from foundation up. The phases of construction, explained

No matter the size, nearly every project moves through the same eight phases in order: design, permitting, site prep and foundation, framing, MEP rough-in, insulation and drywall, finishes, and inspections with final sign-off. Understanding this sequence tells you where you are in the process and why certain steps cannot be rushed or reordered - you cannot close up walls before the rough-in passes inspection, for example.

PhaseWhat happens
1. DesignArchitecture, interior design, and 3D visualization define the plan and finishes.
2. PermittingPlans are submitted to the city; simple work is over-the-counter, larger work goes to plan review.
3. Site prep & foundationThe site is cleared and prepped; footings and foundation are poured.
4. FramingWalls, floors, and roof structure go up - the shape of the project appears.
5. MEP rough-inMechanical, electrical, and plumbing lines are run inside the open framing.
6. Insulation & drywallWalls are insulated and closed up once the rough-in passes inspection.
7. FinishesFlooring, cabinetry, tile, paint, fixtures, and trim complete the space.
8. Inspections & final sign-offInspectors check the work throughout, then issue the final approval.

A small remodel compresses these phases into weeks and skips heavy site work; a custom home stretches each one out and adds more inspection points. But the order holds either way, and inspections happen during construction as well as at the end. Since January 1, 2026, the 2025 California Building Codes have been in effect, so plans and finishes must meet current standards - one more reason the design and permitting phases set the stage for a clean build. Our architecture service and permitting service handle the first two phases so the build phases start on solid footing.

What causes construction delays?

The single most common cause of delay is permitting, because it sits between design and any physical work and its length depends on the city's queue and the project's complexity. In San Jose you apply at SJPermits.org, and the timeline splits sharply by scope: simple like-for-like work can be over-the-counter (same day to about a week), residential plan review commonly runs 3-8 weeks, and additions or new construction run 2-4 months. Planning around that reality - rather than being surprised by it - is what keeps a project on track.

After permits, the usual culprits are material and product lead times, mid-project change orders, and inspection scheduling. Change orders are the most avoidable: every time a decision is revisited after work has started, it ripples through the trades that follow. That is why a well-developed design up front, with finishes selected before construction begins, does more to protect a schedule than any amount of pushing on site. Choosing the right team matters too - in California, verify an active CSLB license (required for any job of $500 or more in combined labor and materials), general liability insurance, workers' comp, bonded status, and a written contract before work starts, and confirm the contractor - not you - pulls the permits. Our building permit timeline guide breaks the approval stage down in detail.

How design-build keeps a project on schedule

Design-build compresses the calendar by putting one team and one contract over both the design and the construction, so the phases overlap instead of stalling at every handoff. In the traditional design-bid-build path, you hire an architect, finish the plans, then competitively bid them to separate general contractors - each transition is a pause and a renegotiation. When the same firm designs and builds, permitting, procurement, and early construction planning can run in parallel with late-stage design, which is the difference between a project that waits and one that moves.

The schedule benefit comes from a few structural advantages: a single point of accountability (no finger-pointing between designer and builder), earlier cost certainty, and fewer change orders - because the builder helped design the project and flagged buildability issues before they became on-site surprises. Design and architecture fees typically run 8-15% of project cost in either model, so the value of design-build is not a cheaper design phase; it is a faster, more predictable overall timeline. Frequently Asked Questions

How long does home construction or a remodel take?

It depends on the project. From design to done, a bathroom takes about 8-14 weeks, a kitchen 12-20 weeks, a home addition 4-8 months, a whole-house remodel 4-9 months, an ADU 6-12 months, and a custom home 12-24 months.

What are the phases of home construction?

Every project moves through the same sequence: design, permitting, site prep and foundation, framing, MEP rough-in (mechanical, electrical, plumbing), insulation and drywall, finishes, then inspections and final sign-off. The design and permitting phases happen before anyone breaks ground.

What causes construction delays?

Permitting is the most common source. In San Jose, over-the-counter permits take a day to about a week, residential plan review commonly runs 3-8 weeks, and additions or new construction run 2-4 months. Material lead times, mid-project change orders, and inspection scheduling add the rest.

How does design-build keep a project on schedule?

Design-build puts one team and one contract over both design and construction, so permitting, procurement, and building overlap instead of waiting on handoffs. That single point of accountability means earlier cost certainty and fewer change orders, because the builder helped design the project.

How long does the permit stage add to a Bay Area build?

In San Jose you apply at SJPermits.org. Simple like-for-like work can be over-the-counter, same day to about a week. Anything structural or that adds square footage needs plan review, commonly 3-8 weeks, with additions and new construction running 2-4 months.

Who handles permits and licensing on a build?

The contractor pulls the permits, not the owner. Verify an active CSLB license, general liability insurance, workers' comp, bonded status, and a written contract before work starts. In California a permit is required for any job of $500 or more in combined labor and materials.
