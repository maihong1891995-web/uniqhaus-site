How to Remodel in a Fire Hazard Severity Zone in the Bay Area

You can remodel in a fire hazard severity zone, but new exterior work must follow California's wildfire construction rules. Since January 1, 2026 they live in the 2025 California Wildland-Urban Interface Code (formerly Chapter 7A). Expect a Class A roof, ember-resistant vents with 1/16 to 1/8 inch mesh, tempered multipane windows, compliant decking within 10 feet of the house, and up to 100 feet of defensible space. Interior-only remodels usually do not trigger these rules. Additions and rebuilds do.

If you plan to remodel in a fire hazard severity zone in the Bay Area, the zone does not stop your project. It changes the exterior materials, the details around vents, eaves, windows and decks, and the paperwork you hand the building department. In Los Gatos, Saratoga, the east and south foothills of San Jose, and Contra Costa towns like Orinda, many homes now sit inside a mapped zone after CAL FIRE updated its local maps in 2025.

This guide explains how to check your address, what actually triggers wildfire construction rules on a remodel, addition or rebuild, which materials each part of the house generally needs, and how to plan all of it into design and budget from day one. It is general information. Your city's building and fire departments have the final word on your parcel.

How do you find out if your home is in a fire hazard severity zone?

It takes about 5 minutes: enter your address in the state map viewer, then confirm with your city. The State Fire Marshal publishes the official Fire Hazard Severity Zone maps, which rank land as Moderate, High or Very High based on fuels, terrain and fire weather.

There are two kinds of maps, and it matters which one covers you:

  • State Responsibility Area (SRA): mostly unincorporated hillside land where the state is responsible for wildfire protection.
  • Local Responsibility Area (LRA): land inside cities and towns. Under SB 63, CAL FIRE now maps all three hazard levels in the LRA, not just Very High. The Santa Clara County Fire Department notes the updated LRA maps were released on February 24, 2025, and each city then adopted them locally.

Local adoption is where the details live:

  • Los Gatos adopted its updated map by Ordinance 2374 on June 17, 2025. The Town's fire hazard map page links the approved map. The highest-hazard areas sit mostly on the southern, hillside side of town.
  • Saratoga lists its WUI map and construction standards on the city's Fire Prevention page, and posts the LRA hazard map as a PDF.
  • San Jose points residents to the county FireSafe Council's interactive map from the Fire Department's wildland-urban interface page. The zones follow the hills along the east and south edges of the city.

Two practical tips. First, check the whole parcel, not just the house pin, because a zone line can cross a lot. Second, ask whether your city also maps a local Wildland-Urban Interface (WUI) area. Some cities apply wildfire construction rules to that boundary, which can reach further than the state map.

Does Chapter 7A apply to a remodel, an addition or a rebuild?

A new home or full rebuild in a High or Very High zone must meet the wildfire construction rules in full. An addition must meet them for the new work. A remodel that stays inside the walls usually does not trigger them. Exterior replacements sit in between and depend on your city.

A quick note on names. For years these rules lived in California Building Code Chapter 7A and the matching California Residential Code section R337, which is why people still search for "Chapter 7A requirements." Starting January 1, 2026, the state moved them into a standalone 2025 California Wildland-Urban Interface Code (Title 24, Part 7). The material standards are familiar. The home in the code book changed.

Under the 2025 WUI Code's administration chapter, the code applies to new residential buildings in the SRA and in LRA areas mapped High or Very High. Additions and alterations are allowed as long as the new work "conforms to that required for a new building," and they cannot make the existing house less safe.

Here is how that usually plays out:

  • Interior remodel (kitchen, baths, layout changes inside the existing shell): wildfire material rules generally do not apply, because you are not building new exterior.
  • Addition: the new walls, roof, vents, windows and any new deck generally need to meet the wildfire standards. Your existing house usually does not have to be retrofitted at the same time, unless your city's amendments say otherwise.
  • Exterior replacement (new roof, new siding, new windows): the new components are commonly reviewed against the wildfire standards. For roofs, state law (Health and Safety Code 13132.7) sets a Class A roof requirement in Very High zones when the roof is replaced, with a threshold tied to how much of the roof you redo.
  • Full rebuild or new custom home: the entire house is designed to the WUI Code from the start.

Cities can add local amendments. Some define a "substantial remodel" by the share of floor area or exterior you touch and then apply the rules to the whole house. That is why we always confirm the trigger with the building department during early design, before drawings are finished.

What does wildfire-resistant construction require, element by element?

Most of the code is about embers, not walls of flame. Embers can travel far ahead of a fire and find their way into vents, gutters, gaps and the space under decks. The table below summarizes what the code generally asks for. Exact options and test standards come from the current WUI Code and your city.

Building elementWhat the code generally requiresDesign notes for a remodel or addition
RoofClass A rated roof assembly in hazard zones.Tile, metal and many asphalt shingles can reach Class A. Plan roof work early, because it often pairs with an addition or a re-roof.
Vents (attic, crawl space, foundation)Listed ember and flame resistant vents, or noncombustible, corrosion resistant mesh with openings between 1/16 inch and 1/8 inch.Listed vents cost more than plain screens but keep airflow. Coordinate count and location with your attic ventilation design.
Eaves and soffitsProtected undersides of eaves using noncombustible, ignition-resistant or fire-retardant-treated materials, or approved assemblies.Enclosed (boxed) eaves are a common choice. Open rafter tails need more detailing.
Exterior walls and sidingNoncombustible or ignition-resistant wall covering, or approved assemblies, from the top of the foundation to the roof.Stucco, fiber cement and some treated wood products are typical. Natural wood siding takes extra care.
Windows, skylights and glazed doorsMultipane glazing with at least one tempered pane, or another approved option such as a 20-minute rated or tested unit.Dual-pane windows with a tempered pane fit most budgets and also help with energy performance.
DecksWalking surfaces within 10 feet of the house must meet wildfire standards. Metal flashing is required where the deck meets the wall.Material choice drives the cost. Keep the space under the deck clear and easy to clean.
GuttersA means to prevent leaves and debris from collecting in the gutter.Gutter guards are simple to add during a roof project. Maintenance still matters.

The 2022 Chapter 7A text is still a useful reference for these details. For example, it sets the 1/16 to 1/8 inch vent mesh range and the 10-foot deck rule. Approved products are often listed by the State Fire Marshal, so ask your designer to specify listed products on the drawings. That saves time at plan check.

What are defensible space and Zone 0 in 2026?

State law already requires up to 100 feet of defensible space around homes in covered areas, or to the property line. Public Resources Code 4291 covers the State Responsibility Area, and Government Code 51182 covers Very High zones in cities. CAL FIRE describes the zones on its defensible space page:

  • Zone 0 (0 to 5 feet): the ember-resistant zone right next to the house.
  • Zone 1 (5 to 30 feet): lean, clean and green. Remove dead plants and keep spacing.
  • Zone 2 (30 to 100 feet): reduce fuel and keep space between trees and shrubs.

Zone 0 status. AB 3074 directed the Board of Forestry to write Zone 0 rules. The Board adopted a Zone 0 package on August 19, 2026. In cities, it targets occupied structures in Very High zones. It covers the 5 feet closest to the house and limits combustible mulch, debris, firewood and new combustible fencing. Board adoption alone does not make the rules enforceable. They also need state legal review and filing, and some parts phase in over several years. At the time of writing we could not confirm that the package was in effect, so check the Board's defensible space zones page for the current status.

For a remodel, Zone 0 is less about paperwork and more about design. A few choices that make sense either way:

  • Use gravel, pavers or concrete next to the house instead of bark mulch.
  • Keep wood fences and gates from attaching directly to the house. Use a metal section where they meet.
  • Design decks so the space underneath can be closed off or kept clear.
  • Plan the landscape at the same time as the building, not after.

How does a fire zone affect insurance and resale?

Two things are clear from official sources. First, the California Department of Insurance's Safer from Wildfires program lists 10 home and community actions that qualify for insurance discounts. They include a Class A roof, ember and fire resistant vents, enclosed eaves, upgraded windows, 6 inches of noncombustible material at the bottom of exterior walls, and a 5-foot ember-resistant zone. Many of these are exactly the items a remodel touches.

Second, when you sell a home in a High or Very High zone, Civil Code 1102.19 requires the seller to give the buyer documentation of defensible space compliance. A final inspection report covering that work can serve this purpose. Keeping permits, product listings and inspection records from your remodel makes that step easier later.

We do not predict premiums or sale prices. Your insurer and agent can tell you which upgrades they recognize. What we can do is document every wildfire-related item in your plans so you have a clear record to share.

How do you plan wildfire requirements into design and budget?

Treat the zone as a design input in week 1, not a surprise at plan check. When it shows up late, it can mean redrawn elevations, a second round of comments, and products that do not match the budget.

  1. Confirm the zone and the trigger. Check the map, then confirm with the building department what your scope triggers. Get it in writing if your city offers that.
  2. Choose the exterior palette with the code in mind. Siding, roof, windows and decking are both a design decision and a code decision. Pick them together.
  3. Put listed products on the drawings. Vents, windows, deck boards and siding should be named, not left as "per code."
  4. Price the exterior in detail. Wildfire-rated choices can shift line items for roofing, vents, windows and decking. An itemized estimate shows exactly where.
  5. Carry a real contingency. We suggest 10 to 20 percent on remodels, especially when you open older exterior walls.
  6. Decide remodel or rebuild with the zone in view. If you are already replacing most of the exterior, the math can lean toward a rebuild. Our remodel vs rebuild guide walks through that choice.

For context, whole-house remodels in the Bay Area typically run $100 to $500 per square foot, with mid-range projects around $200 to $400. Custom homes typically run $400 to $700 or more per square foot. Fire zone details are part of those numbers, not a separate project. That is why we price them inside the same estimate.

Lots in foothill towns are often sloped as well, which brings its own structural and drainage planning. That is a separate topic from wildfire materials, so plan for both.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if my house is in a fire hazard severity zone in California?

Enter your address in the State Fire Marshal's Fire Hazard Severity Zone map, then confirm with your city's building or fire department. Cities adopted updated local maps in 2025, and some also map a local WUI area. Check the whole parcel, since a zone line can cross a lot.

Do I have to upgrade my whole house to fire code if I remodel the kitchen?

Usually not. An interior remodel inside the existing walls does not normally trigger wildfire material rules. New exterior work, such as an addition, new windows or a new roof, is what typically gets reviewed. Some cities apply the rules to the whole house above a set remodel size, so confirm with your building department.

Is Chapter 7A still the code for wildfire zones in 2026?

The same material standards now sit in the 2025 California Wildland-Urban Interface Code, Title 24 Part 7, which took effect January 1, 2026. Before that they were in Building Code Chapter 7A and Residential Code R337. Many contractors and city pages still call them Chapter 7A.

Is Zone 0 required in California right now?

The Board of Forestry adopted Zone 0 rules for the 5 feet around homes on August 19, 2026, but adoption alone does not make them enforceable, and parts phase in over years. Check the Board of Forestry's defensible space page for current status. Zone 1 and Zone 2 defensible space out to 100 feet already applies in covered areas.

What windows are required in a high fire hazard zone?

The code generally calls for multipane glazing with at least one tempered pane, or another approved option such as a tested or 20-minute rated unit. Dual-pane windows with a tempered pane are a common, budget-friendly choice. Your city may list accepted products.

Do fire-resistant upgrades lower home insurance in California?

Under the Department of Insurance's Safer from Wildfires program, actions like a Class A roof, ember-resistant vents, enclosed eaves and upgraded windows qualify for discounts. The amount depends on your insurer, so ask your agent which upgrades they recognize.

Key Takeaways

  • Since January 1, 2026, wildfire construction rules are in the 2025 California Wildland-Urban Interface Code, which carries forward the old Chapter 7A standards.
  • New homes, rebuilds and additions in High or Very High zones must meet the code for the new work. Interior-only remodels usually do not.
  • Key specs: Class A roof, vent mesh between 1/16 and 1/8 inch, a tempered pane in windows, and compliant decking within 10 feet of the house.
  • Defensible space reaches up to 100 feet. Zone 0 covers the first 5 feet, and its status should be checked before you finalize landscape plans.
