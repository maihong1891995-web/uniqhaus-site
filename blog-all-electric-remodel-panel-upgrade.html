Going All-Electric in a Bay Area Remodel: Panel Size, PG&E, and Planning

Many Bay Area homes with 100 to 150 amps can go all-electric with planning, using load management and lower-amp appliances. A whole house remodel that adds space, a heat pump, induction, and a Level 2 EV charger often lands on 200-amp service, which may require a PG&E upgrade that can take months. Get a load calculation during design and start the utility application early. If your home also needs new wiring, see our rewiring and repiping guide.

An all-electric remodel in the Bay Area usually comes down to one question: can your electrical service carry a heat pump, an induction range, a heat pump water heater, and an EV charger at the same time? In San Jose and across the South Bay, many homes built in the 1950s through 1970s still run on 100-amp service, and the answer depends on a load calculation, not a guess. This guide walks through how to plan the electrical side of going all-electric during a remodel, from appliance loads and panel size to the PG&E service upgrade, permits, and what drives the cost.

If your remodel also needs new wiring or new supply pipe throughout, read our guide to rewiring and repiping in a whole house remodel first. This article focuses on capacity: how much power the house needs, and how to get it.

Do you need a 200-amp panel to go all-electric?

Not always. Rewiring America, a nonprofit focused on home electrification, says most homes with 100 to 150 amps can be fully electrified with some planning, and homes above 150 amps usually need less careful planning (see its panel upgrade guide and 100-amp electrification guide). Homes with 40 or 60 amp service almost always need an upgrade.

In practice, the remodel itself changes the math. A small kitchen refresh with an induction range rarely forces a service upgrade. A whole house remodel that adds square footage, a second HVAC zone, a home office, a Level 2 charger, and an induction range is a different situation. For a typical tech household with one or two EVs, 200-amp service is a common target, but it is a result of the load calculation, not a default.

The goal is the right capacity for your actual loads. Oversizing costs money and can trigger a utility upgrade you did not need. Undersizing means tripped breakers, or a second electrical project two years after you move back in.

How much power does each electric appliance need?

Most major electric appliances need a dedicated 240-volt circuit, usually between 30 and 60 amps. The table below shows common circuit sizes. Treat it as a planning starting point only. The real number comes from the nameplate and installation manual of the exact model you choose, and your electrician sizes each circuit to code.

Two things stand out for Bay Area tech households. First, the EV charger is often the single largest new load, and many owners do not need 48 amps. Rewiring America notes that even a 16-amp Level 2 charger can add 100 or more miles of range overnight. Second, choosing a few lower-amp appliances early in design can be the difference between keeping your existing service and waiting on the utility.

100A vs 200A: what the load calculation decides

The load calculation is the document that settles the panel question. It follows Article 220 of the electrical code and adds up the home's square footage, kitchen and laundry circuits, fixed appliances, HVAC, and EV charging, then applies the demand factors the code allows. For an existing home, the code also allows an electrician to use a year of actual peak demand data from your utility meter as the starting point, which often shows more headroom than people expect.

The code itself changed recently. The 2025 California Electrical Code, based on the 2023 National Electrical Code, took effect statewide on January 1, 2026 (see the California Building Standards Commission). The 2023 NEC added rules that let a service be sized around an energy management system that caps total current. How your city applies those provisions to a specific project is up to the building department, so ask before you design around them.

Here is how the result usually plays out:

  • Calculated load fits the existing service. You may only need a new or larger panel (the box inside your home), new circuits, and possibly a subpanel. PG&E may not be involved beyond a disconnect and reconnect.
  • Calculated load is slightly over. Load management, circuit sharing, or a few lower-amp appliance choices can often bring it back under.
  • Calculated load is well over, or you are adding a lot of space. You need a service upgrade, which means PG&E replaces or upsizes the line, meter, and sometimes the transformer that serves your home.

Ask for the load calculation during design, before you pick appliances. It costs little and it shapes every electrical decision after it.

Can a smart panel or load management avoid a service upgrade?

Often, yes. The City of Palo Alto's utility guidance on avoiding a panel upgrade describes several options that let a home add electric loads without upsizing service:

  • Circuit sharing. Two large appliances share one circuit, but only one runs at a time. The common example is an EV charger that pauses while the dryer runs.
  • Smart panels and smart breakers. They monitor real-time use and shed or delay loads, such as EV charging, to keep the home under its limit.
  • Demand management devices. These can reach similar results at a lower cost than a full smart panel.
  • Subpanels and tandem breakers. They add circuit space, which solves a "no room in the box" problem, but not a capacity problem.

Load management works best when the household can live with small tradeoffs, such as the car charging overnight rather than at full speed the moment you plug in. It is less useful when you are adding a large addition or an ADU, where the base load itself grows. A licensed electrician must confirm any of these setups is compatible and code compliant, and the inspector has the final say.

How long does a PG&E service upgrade take?

Plan for months, not weeks. Rewiring America notes that in some utility regions a service upgrade can mean months-long delays, and that a utility service or transformer upgrade can push costs to roughly $5,000 to $25,000, with underground service lines among the most expensive cases (source). PG&E handles residential service changes through its online residential project resources and project application process.

The typical sequence looks like this:

  1. Your electrician completes the load calculation and decides on service size.
  2. An application is submitted to PG&E with the requested load and panel location.
  3. PG&E reviews, designs its side of the work, and quotes any fees you owe.
  4. Your contractor installs the new meter and panel equipment under a city electrical permit.
  5. The city inspects and releases the work to the utility.
  6. PG&E schedules the cutover and energizes the new service.

The timeline risk sits in steps 3 and 6, which your contractor does not control. That is why the PG&E application should start during design, not after demolition. In a whole house remodel that runs 6 to 12 months, an early application usually lets the utility work run in parallel with framing and rough-in instead of holding up the finish phase. For more on schedule risk, see our guide to hidden costs in a whole house remodel.

Is all-electric required for a remodel in San Jose?

For most remodels, no. San Jose's reach code targets new construction, and the city's own FAQs state that its all-electric provisions do not apply to additions or alterations. Remodels still have to meet the statewide Title 24 energy code for the systems you change. Rules get updated, so confirm the current version for your address before design is final.

A regional rule is the bigger driver for remodels. The Bay Area Air District's Rules 9-4 and 9-6 set zero-NOx standards for new appliances: January 1, 2027 for typical residential tank water heaters, and January 1, 2029 for furnaces. The district says these apply to appliances manufactured after those dates, and only when you replace equipment, not to working appliances already in place. The Air District board can still revise timelines, but if your remodel replaces a water heater or furnace anyway, planning electric capacity for both now avoids redoing the panel later.

On permits: a panel change, service upgrade, new circuits, and EV charger all need an electrical permit and inspection. In San Jose, permits are filed through SJPermits.org. In a remodel, this work usually rides on the same permit set as the rest of the project. Our San Jose building permit guide explains the steps.

What incentives are still available in 2026?

Fewer than in past years, so do not build the budget around them. The federal home improvement credit for heat pumps and panels (Section 25C) does not apply to property placed in service after December 31, 2025, per the IRS. The federal home EV charger credit (Section 30C) ended for property placed in service after June 30, 2026, per the IRS.

California's HEEHRA rebates, run through TECH Clean California, were fully reserved for single-family homes statewide as of February 24, 2026, with new applications placed on a waitlist. Programs reopen and change, so check the TECH Clean California HEEHRA page and your local energy provider directly before you plan on any rebate. Treat any incentive as a bonus, not a line in your budget.

Why do the electrical upgrade during a remodel?

Because the expensive part of electrical work in a finished home is access. When walls and ceilings are already open, new circuits for the range, water heater, heat pump, and garage charger run in a straight line to where the new design needs them. There is no separate patch-and-paint bill, and it is one permit and one inspection round instead of several.

The main cost drivers for the electrical side of an all-electric remodel are:

  • Service vs panel. A panel swap on existing service costs far less than a utility service upgrade. Our rewiring guide puts a 100 to 200 amp panel upgrade at roughly $3,500 to $8,000 in the Bay Area; utility-side work comes on top of that.
  • Overhead vs underground service. Trenching and underground conductors add cost and time.
  • Distance and routing. A charger 60 feet from the panel, or a heat pump on the far side of the house, means longer and heavier wire runs.
  • Existing wiring condition. Older branch wiring may need replacing anyway once the walls are open.
  • Appliance choices. Lower-amp equipment can keep you on existing service.

Sequencing matters as much as cost. The order that works: load calculation and appliance selection in design, PG&E application before permits are issued, rough electrical after framing, then inspection before insulation and drywall. Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a panel upgrade cost in the Bay Area?

Our rewiring guide puts a 100 to 200 amp panel upgrade at roughly $3,500 to $8,000 in the Bay Area, scaled to home size. If PG&E must upgrade the service line or transformer, Rewiring America estimates total costs can reach about $5,000 to $25,000, with underground service among the most expensive cases.

Can I run a heat pump, induction stove, and EV charger on 100 amps?

Sometimes. Rewiring America says most homes with 100 to 150 amps can fully electrify with planning, often by using a lower-amp EV charger setting, a 120V or 15A heat pump water heater, and circuit sharing. A load calculation by a licensed electrician is the only way to know for your home.

How long does PG&E take to upgrade electrical service?

It varies by location and workload, and Rewiring America notes some utility regions see months-long delays. Start the PG&E application during design, so utility work runs in parallel with construction instead of holding up the finish phase.

Does San Jose require all-electric appliances in a remodel?

For most remodels, no. San Jose's reach code targets new construction, and city FAQs say its all-electric provisions do not apply to additions or alterations. Regional Air District rules for new gas water heaters (2027) and furnaces (2029) apply when equipment is replaced, so check current rules before you finalize design.

Do I need a permit to install an EV charger in San Jose?

Yes. A Level 2 charger circuit needs an electrical permit and inspection, and in San Jose permits are filed through SJPermits.org. During a remodel, the charger circuit is usually included in the project's electrical scope and permit.

Is a smart panel worth it instead of a service upgrade?

It can be, when your calculated load is only slightly over your service size. Smart panels and circuit-sharing devices shed or delay loads such as EV charging to stay under the limit. They help less when a large addition or ADU raises the base load, where a service upgrade is often the cleaner answer.

Key Takeaways

  • Many homes with 100 to 150 amps can go all-electric with planning; the load calculation, not a rule of thumb, decides if you need 200 amps.
  • A Level 2 EV charger is often the largest new load: a 48A charger needs a 60A circuit, but lower settings can avoid a service upgrade.
  • Start the PG&E service application during design; utility timelines can run months and sit outside your contractor's control.
  • Federal credits 25C and 30C ended for property placed in service after Dec 31, 2025 and June 30, 2026, so budget without them.
