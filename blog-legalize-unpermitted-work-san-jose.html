How to Legalize Unpermitted Work in San Jose

How to Legalize Unpermitted Work in San Jose

Most unpermitted work in San Jose can be legalized by filing a permit for the existing work through SJPermits.org with as-built drawings, then passing inspections that may require opening walls. The work must meet current code, and a sale does not make it legal. Unpermitted ADUs built before January 1, 2020 have extra protection under state law, and San Jose says its AB 2533 program typically takes 4 to 6 months from a complete application.

If you want to legalize unpermitted work in San Jose, you are not alone and you are not stuck. Many San Jose homes, especially older houses and fixer-uppers bought in the last few years, come with a converted garage, a back bedroom, or a bathroom that never went through the city. The good news is that there is a defined path: the work is documented, reviewed against today's code, inspected, and signed off, just like new work. This guide covers that path, what the city tends to check, and your realistic options.

One thing first, because many owners feel embarrassed about it: if you bought the house this way, you did not cause the problem. The goal now is simply to get a clean, documented record you can live with, refinance with, or sell with.

Can you legalize unpermitted work in San Jose?

Yes. In most cases, unpermitted work in San Jose can be legalized by applying for a building permit for the existing work and bringing it up to current standards. The city is direct about the ground rules. Its building bulletin on unpermitted work explains that construction done without required permits violates the State Building Code, that work completed without permits must meet current zoning and building standards to be legalized, and that a change of ownership does not make unpermitted work legal. In plain terms, the responsibility passes to whoever owns the house today, even if the work is decades old.

The same bulletin also carries the most reassuring line for owners: property owners who voluntarily seek compliance for unpermitted improvements may avoid a citation. Coming forward on your own terms, before a complaint, a sale, or a refinance forces the issue, generally gives you more control over timing and scope.

If you are not sure where to begin, the city's Legalizing Construction page invites owners to speak with a Code Enforcement Inspector at (408) 535-7770 about which permits are needed and how to legalize unpermitted construction. Applications themselves are filed through SJPermits.org, the same portal used for new projects. For how normal permits move through review, our San Jose building permit process guide covers the basics; this article focuses only on work that already exists.

The legalization path, step by step

Legalizing existing work usually follows eight steps, and the first three happen before anything is filed with the city. The order matters because each step feeds the next.

  1. Assess what was actually done. Walk the house with someone who can read construction: what was built, what was changed, and what is concealed behind finishes. A garage conversion, for example, may involve framing, electrical, plumbing, insulation, a new window, and a changed use, all at once.
  2. Pull the permit history. Compare what the city has on file with what exists. Sometimes part of the work was permitted and part was not, or an old permit was opened and never finaled. Knowing which pieces are missing keeps the scope honest.
  3. Prepare as-built drawings. The city reviews plans, not photos. As-built drawings show the existing condition accurately: floor plans, the structure, electrical and plumbing layouts, and any change in use. If you also want to change things, this is where design decisions start.
  4. Bring in engineering if structure is involved. Removed walls, added rooms, new beams, decks, and second-story work often need structural calculations from an engineer. Energy compliance under California's Title 24 energy standards may also apply to conditioned space that was added or converted.
  5. Submit the application. The package goes in through SJPermits.org. Smaller scopes may qualify for faster review; anything with structural changes typically goes through plan review.
  6. Respond to plan check comments. Reviewers may ask for more detail or for changes to bring the work up to current code. Answer them completely the first time to avoid extra review rounds.
  7. Inspections, often with openings. Because the work is already finished, inspectors usually need to see what is behind the drywall. Expect targeted openings at framing connections, electrical, plumbing, or insulation. Sometimes a few small cuts are enough; sometimes a whole wall comes down.
  8. Correct and final. Anything that does not meet code gets fixed, re-inspected, and closed out. The final sign-off is the document you want in your files for a lender, an appraiser, or a buyer.

What the city usually checks, by type of work

Every case is different, but most unpermitted work in San Jose homes falls into seven common types, and each tends to raise the same questions. The table below is a general guide, not a city checklist; the actual requirements depend on what your plans show and what the inspector finds.

The risk is rarely the visible finish. It is the structure, wiring, and water management you cannot see, often left by an unreliable contractor or a quick flip. Legalization is when those shortcuts get found and fixed.

Legalize, remove, or sell as-is?

You have three realistic options, and the right one depends on what the space is worth to you and how far it is from code. Here is how they compare.

OptionBest whenTrade-offs
LegalizeThe space adds real value (a bedroom, a bathroom, an ADU) and the construction is mostly soundDrawings, possible engineering, permit fees, openings, and corrective work. You end with a documented, insurable space.
Remove or restoreThe work is poorly built, cannot meet zoning, or costs more to fix than it is worthYou lose the space, and removal itself may need a permit. You also clear the problem cleanly.
Sell as-is with disclosureYou need to sell soon and do not want to take on constructionBuyers and lenders may discount the price or treat the space as non-existent. Disclosure is still required.

On disclosure: California's Real Estate Transfer Disclosure Statement, set out in Civil Code section 1102.6, asks sellers whether they are aware of room additions, structural modifications, or other alterations or repairs made without necessary permits, and whether any work is not in compliance with building codes. If you know about unpermitted work, plan on disclosing it. How to word that disclosure, and what it means for your sale, is a question for a real estate attorney or your agent. We are builders, and this is not legal advice.

The San Jose bulletin adds one more reason to take legalization seriously: it warns that insurance may not cover unpermitted improvements. If the converted garage is where your family sleeps, that alone may tip the decision.

Unpermitted ADUs have their own state-law paths

If the unpermitted space is an ADU or JADU built before January 1, 2020, California law gives you more protection than for other unpermitted work. Two laws matter.

The AB 2533 legalization path

Under Government Code section 66311.7 (formerly section 66332, expanded by AB 2533 in 2024), a local agency generally cannot deny a permit for an unpermitted ADU or JADU constructed before January 1, 2020, just because it does not meet current building standards or certain ADU rules. The key exception is a unit that is substandard under Health and Safety Code section 17920.3, meaning it poses real health and safety problems; those conditions still have to be corrected. The law also limits certain fees for these units, so impact fees generally do not apply.

San Jose runs a dedicated AB 2533 ADU and JADU Legalization Program. The city asks owners to gather records showing the unit was built or converted before January 1, 2020, points to its FAQ and substandard housing checklist for acceptable evidence, and accepts applications by email to the ADU Ally Team. The city describes the process as typically taking about 4 to 6 months from a complete application. Check the program page for current forms before you apply, since details can change.

The five-year delay in enforcement

Separately, Health and Safety Code section 17980.12, added by SB 13 in 2019, lets the owner of a qualifying ADU who receives a notice to correct violations request a five-year delay in enforcement. The agency must grant it if correcting the violation is not necessary to protect health and safety. Applications cannot be approved on or after January 1, 2030, and the section is set to expire in 2035. A delay is breathing room, not a permit; most owners who plan to rent, refinance, or sell will still want the unit fully legalized.

What drives the cost of legalizing work

There is no honest flat price for legalization, because the cost depends almost entirely on what the inspector finds once walls are open. We would rather explain the drivers than quote a number that does not fit your house. Five things move the budget:

  • Drawings. As-built plans are required, and the more complex the work, the more detailed they need to be.
  • Engineering. Structural calculations add cost when walls were removed, rooms added, or decks built.
  • Permit and review fees. These are set by the city and scale with the project. Fees for work already done without a permit can differ from a standard permit, so confirm current amounts with the Building Division before you budget. Qualifying pre-2020 ADUs are protected from impact fees under state law.
  • Openings and patching. Cutting drywall for inspection, then repairing and repainting it.
  • Corrective work. This is the true unknown. A sound conversion may need small fixes; a poorly built addition may need new framing, wiring, or a foundation upgrade.

Because of that last item, we recommend carrying a 10 to 20 percent contingency on any legalization scope. And because surprises usually live behind walls, our fixer-upper renovation guide is worth reading if you recently bought the house.

Combine legalization with your planned remodel

If you already plan to remodel, legalizing at the same time is usually the most efficient path, because one set of drawings, one review, and one round of inspections can cover both. You are going to open walls for the remodel anyway, so the inspector can see concealed work without extra demolition. The old space is brought to code as part of a design you actually want, instead of being fixed in place and remodeled again later.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I get a permit for work that was already done in San Jose?

Yes. You apply for a permit for the existing work with as-built drawings, and the city reviews and inspects it like new work. San Jose says work done without permits must meet current zoning and building standards to be legalized, so some corrections are common.

Will the city fine me for coming forward about unpermitted work?

San Jose states that property owners who voluntarily seek compliance for unpermitted improvements may avoid a citation. Fees for work already done can differ from a standard permit, so confirm current amounts with the Building Division before you budget.

Do I have to open walls to legalize unpermitted work?

Often, yes. Inspectors need to see concealed framing, wiring, plumbing, and insulation, so expect at least targeted openings. The extent depends on the type of work and what the inspector needs to verify.

Do I have to disclose unpermitted work when selling a house in California?

The Transfer Disclosure Statement under Civil Code 1102.6 asks sellers about additions, structural changes, or repairs made without necessary permits. If you know about it, plan to disclose it. For how to word it, talk to a real estate attorney or your agent.

How do I legalize an unpermitted garage conversion in San Jose?

Document the conversion with as-built drawings, file through SJPermits.org, and expect review of the change of use, egress, insulation, energy compliance, and electrical. If it is used as an ADU and was converted before January 1, 2020, San Jose's AB 2533 program may apply.

Does buying a house with unpermitted work make it legal?

No. San Jose's guidance says a change of ownership does not make unpermitted work legal, and the current owner is responsible for correcting it. That is true even if a previous owner or their contractor did the work.

Key Takeaways

  • Unpermitted work in San Jose can usually be legalized, but it must meet current code, and a sale does not make it legal.
  • Expect 8 steps: assess, pull records, as-built drawings, engineering, submit, plan check, inspections with openings, and final.
  • Unpermitted ADUs and JADUs built before January 1, 2020 have state-law protection; San Jose cites about 4 to 6 months for its AB 2533 program.
  • Carry a 10 to 20 percent contingency, and combine legalization with a planned remodel to use one set of drawings and one review.
