How Long Does It Take to Build a Custom Home in the Bay Area?

A realistic 2026 timeline from feasibility and design through permits and construction - plus the factors that stretch a custom home schedule and how to plan around them.

One of the first questions we hear from families planning a new home is simple to ask and harder to answer: how long does it take to build a custom home in the Bay Area? The honest answer is that it takes longer than most people expect, because a custom home is not a single build but a sequence of design, approval, and construction phases that each take real time. The good news is that the timeline is predictable once you understand the phases and what drives each one. This guide lays out a realistic 2026 schedule, from your first feasibility conversation through permits and construction, and shows where the weeks tend to disappear. It pairs with our steps to build a new home in the Bay Area and our custom homes service.

The total timeline: a realistic 18 to 30 months

For a typical Bay Area custom home, plan on a realistic range of about 18 to 30 months from the moment you start feasibility work to the day you move in. That is a planning window rather than a promise, and where you land inside it depends on your lot, your city, and how complex the design is. A straightforward home on a flat, ready-to-build lot in a fast-permitting city can come in near the bottom of the range, while a larger home on a hillside lot, in a jurisdiction with design review, sits closer to the top.

It helps to think of the total as three stacked phases rather than one long build. Design and engineering usually run 6 to 12 months, permits and plan check add 3 to 8 months, and construction takes 10 to 16 months. Those windows overlap somewhat when the project is run well, which is why the total is not a simple sum of the maximums. The mistake most homeowners make is picturing only the construction phase and assuming the whole project fits inside it. In practice, roughly half the calendar is spent before anyone breaks ground, and understanding that up front is the difference between a schedule you can plan around and one that feels like a series of surprises.

Phase-by-phase breakdown

Each phase has its own rhythm and its own risks. Below is how the calendar typically breaks down for a Bay Area custom home, followed by a closer look at feasibility and design, permits and plan check, and construction. Read the durations as realistic planning ranges, not fixed guarantees, because every lot and every jurisdiction is different.

PhaseTypical durationWhat happens
Feasibility and pre-design1 to 3 monthsSite study, budget alignment, zoning and setback checks, and confirming what the lot will allow
Design and engineering6 to 12 monthsSchematic design, design development, structural and MEP engineering, and permit-ready construction drawings
Permits and plan check3 to 8 monthsCity submittal, plan check corrections, any design review or HOA approval, and permit issuance
Construction10 to 16 monthsSite work and foundation, framing, systems rough-in, finishes, inspections, and final sign-off

Feasibility and design. The earliest phase sets the tempo for everything that follows. Feasibility work confirms what your lot will actually allow under current zoning, setbacks, and site conditions, and aligns the design ambition with a realistic budget before anyone draws a floor plan. From there, design moves through schematic design, design development, and finally the detailed construction documents that a city will accept. This is also where structural and mechanical engineering happen. The phase feels slow because it is the phase where decisions are cheap to change, and every choice deferred here becomes far more expensive later. Families who move quickly and decisively through design almost always finish the whole project sooner.

Permits and plan check. Once the drawings are complete, they go to the city for review. Bay Area plan check commonly takes 3 to 8 months, and longer where design review boards, HOA approval, or environmental factors apply. This is the phase homeowners control the least, because it depends on the jurisdiction and its workload. What you do control is the quality of the submittal: complete, coordinated drawings that answer the reviewer's questions the first time avoid the correction rounds that add months. A separate look at what it costs to build a custom home in the Bay Area shows how permit and engineering fees fit the larger budget.

Construction. With a permit issued, the build itself typically runs 10 to 16 months. Site work and the foundation come first, followed by framing, then the rough-in of plumbing, electrical, and HVAC, then insulation, drywall, and the long finishing stretch of cabinetry, tile, flooring, and fixtures. Inspections punctuate the whole sequence. Bigger homes, complex foundations on sloped lots, and high-end finish packages all push toward the top of the range, while a clean design on a flat lot moves faster.

What slows a custom home down

Most timeline overruns are not mysteries. They come from a short list of recurring causes, and nearly all of them are manageable when they are planned for early rather than discovered mid-project. Knowing where the weeks tend to disappear is the best way to protect your schedule.

Permits and plan check. The single most common source of delay is the review process, especially when incomplete or uncoordinated drawings trigger multiple correction rounds. Each round can add weeks. HOA or design review approval, where it applies, runs on its own committee calendar and can add months that have nothing to do with the city. Mid-project design changes are the next big culprit: deciding to move a wall or upgrade a kitchen after construction has started ripples through ordering, framing, and inspections. Material lead times matter more than people expect, since specialty windows, custom cabinetry, and imported finishes can carry lead times of many weeks or months and must be ordered well ahead. Finally, weather affects the early exterior work, and a wet Bay Area winter can slow site work and foundations. The pattern across all of these is that surprises cost the most, and a project planned to anticipate them holds its schedule far better. How design-build shortens the timeline

The way a project is structured has a real effect on how long it takes. In the traditional model, you hire an architect, wait for the design to finish, then bid the completed drawings out to separate general contractors, and only then start construction. That hand-off creates a gap, and it often surfaces budget problems late, when redesigning to fit the number costs weeks or months. A design-build firm shortens the timeline by putting design, engineering, and construction under one team and one contract, so those phases overlap instead of stacking end to end.

Because the people who design the home are the same people who price and build it, several things happen earlier. Long lead materials get identified and ordered during design rather than after permit issuance. Construction drawings tend to be more complete and better coordinated, which cuts plan check correction rounds. Pre-construction planning runs in parallel with the final design instead of starting from scratch after a separate bid. None of this removes the city's review time, which is outside anyone's control, but it compresses the parts of the schedule you do control. How to plan realistically

The families who have the smoothest experience are the ones who plan to the real timeline instead of a hoped-for one. Start early, and start the feasibility and budget conversation before you are emotionally committed to a specific move-in date. If you are selling a current home or coordinating school years, build the full 18 to 30 month window into that plan and add a buffer rather than assuming the fast end.

Make design decisions promptly and try to hold them, because indecision and mid-project changes are the two things most likely to stretch a schedule you would otherwise control. Ask your team to order long lead items early and to submit complete, coordinated drawings the first time. Keep the budget realistic from the start so you are not forced into a redesign that costs months. Most of all, treat the durations in this guide as planning ranges to prepare for, not guarantees to hold anyone to, since every lot and jurisdiction differs. Frequently Asked Questions

How long does it take to build a custom home in the Bay Area?

For most projects, plan on a realistic range of about 18 to 30 months from your first feasibility conversation to move-in. That span usually breaks down into roughly 6 to 12 months for design and engineering, 3 to 8 months for permits and plan check, and 10 to 16 months of construction. Lot conditions, jurisdiction, and design complexity move the range, so treat these as planning windows rather than guarantees.

How long does construction take once the permit is issued?

Construction alone typically runs about 10 to 16 months once the permit is issued and you break ground. Larger homes, hillside or complex foundations, and high-end finishes push toward the top of that range or beyond, while a straightforward flat lot with a clear design can finish faster. Weather, material lead times, and inspection scheduling all affect the on-site pace.

How long do permits take for a custom home?

Permitting and plan check in the Bay Area commonly take about 3 to 8 months, and sometimes longer where design review, HOA approval, or environmental factors apply. Timelines vary widely by jurisdiction, so a city with online plan check and clear rules moves faster than one with multiple review rounds. Submitting complete, coordinated drawings the first time is the single biggest thing you control here.

What causes the biggest delays when building a custom home?

The most common causes are permit and plan check delays, HOA or design review approvals, mid-project design changes, long material lead times, and weather. Incomplete or uncoordinated drawings that trigger extra correction rounds are a frequent hidden cause. Each of these is manageable when it is planned for early rather than discovered during the build.

Does design-build make building a custom home faster?

A design-build firm can shorten the overall timeline by overlapping design, engineering, and pre-construction planning under one team and one contract. Because the people who design the home also price and build it, material lead items get ordered early, permit-ready drawings are more complete, and there is no separate re-bidding gap between design and construction. It does not remove city review time, but it reduces the delays you control.

Key Takeaways

  • Plan on a realistic 18 to 30 month window from feasibility to move-in, not just the construction phase.
  • The calendar stacks into roughly 6 to 12 months of design, 3 to 8 months of permits, and 10 to 16 months of construction.
  • The biggest delays come from plan check corrections, HOA or design review, mid-project changes, material lead times, and weather.
  • Design-build overlaps phases and orders long lead items early, compressing the parts of the schedule you control.
