Whole House Remodel: What to Expect Month by Month

A whole house remodel typically spans 6 to 12 months: roughly months 1 to 3 for design and permitting, then demolition, structure, and rough systems, then insulation and drywall, then finishes, with final inspection at the end. Each phase has to finish before the next starts, because later work covers up what came before.

A whole house remodel feels less overwhelming when you can see it as a sequence of months rather than one long stretch of disruption. The whole house remodel process moves in a fixed order set by construction logic, and knowing what each month brings helps you plan life around the work and spot when a phase is slipping. This guide walks the project month by month, from the first design meeting to move-in. It pairs with our detailed whole house remodel timeline and the guide on where to start.

What to expect month by month

A whole house remodel usually runs 6 to 12 months from the first design meeting to move-in, with the exact length driven by the size of the home, the depth of the scope, and how fast permits clear. The schedule below shows the typical phases and roughly when each one happens.

Month(s)PhaseWhat happens
1 to 3Design & permitsDesign, construction drawings, engineering, and plan check with the City of San Jose
Start of buildDemolitionRemove finishes and anything being replaced; expose structure and systems
Early buildStructureFoundation work, framing, load-bearing changes, and any additions
Early buildRough systemsElectrical, plumbing, and HVAC roughed in while walls are open
Mid buildInsulation & drywallClose the walls once rough inspections pass
Later buildFinishesFlooring, cabinets, tile, paint, and fixtures
Final monthFinal & move-inFinal inspection, punch list, closeout, and move back in

The months are approximate, not fixed dates. A modest cosmetic-heavy remodel lands near the shorter end, while a down-to-studs gut with additions and structural changes runs to the longer end. What does not change is the order: anything that gets covered up goes in first, and inspections gate the move from one phase to the next.

Months 1 to 3: design and permitting

The first phase, about months 1 to 3, is design and permitting - and no walls come down yet. This is where goals turn into a design, a design turns into construction drawings, and those drawings go through plan check. It is the least visible phase and the one that protects the entire budget and schedule.

Work in this stretch typically includes:

  • Design and space planning. Layouts, elevations, and 3D views so decisions get made on screen before they cost money in the field - the core of our design package.
  • Engineering and construction drawings. Structural engineering and the detailed drawings a permit reviewer and a build crew both need.
  • Plan check with the City of San Jose. Permit submittal and review, filed at SJPermits.org. Permitting is the variable that moves the schedule most, so we submit early and run drawings and review in parallel rather than one after the other.

Because permitting timing is unpredictable, this phase can be shorter or longer than three months. Front-loading it is how you keep the construction months from stalling - the full breakdown lives in our architecture and permitting service.

Early construction: demolition, structure, and systems

Once permits are approved, the early construction months cover demolition, structure, and rough systems - the phases that are invisible in the finished home but decide whether everything on top of them holds up. This is the loudest, dustiest stretch of the project.

It moves in this order:

  • Demolition. The first physical step on site. Finishes and anything being replaced come out, exposing the structure and systems to work on.
  • Structure. Foundation work, framing, load-bearing changes, and any additions. Everything else attaches to a sound structure, so it comes before systems.
  • Rough systems. Electrical, plumbing, and HVAC roughed into the open walls. These must be in and pass a rough inspection before anything closes the walls.

Older pre-1980 Bay Area homes often reveal surprises once walls are open - dated wiring, corroded plumbing, or framing that needs repair. This is why a 10 to 20 percent contingency matters: it keeps the schedule moving instead of stalling when the unexpected shows up. For how the phases connect end to end, see our construction and remodel timeline.

Later construction: drywall and finishes

After rough inspections pass, the later construction months close the walls and add every visible finish. This is when the home starts to look like the design instead of a job site.

The sequence here is:

  • Insulation and drywall. Only after rough electrical, plumbing, and HVAC are approved do the walls get insulated and closed with drywall.
  • Finishes. Flooring, cabinets, tile, paint, and fixtures go on last, over finished walls. This is the layer homeowners picture first, but it is deliberately the final construction phase.

Finishes often take longer than expected because they involve the most selections and the most trades - cabinet installers, tile setters, painters, and fixture crews working in sequence. Having selections locked before this phase begins is what keeps it on schedule, since a single back-ordered item can hold up a room.

Final month: inspection, punch list, and move-in

The last phase is final inspection, a punch list, and move-in. It is short but important, because it is where the project gets signed off and handed back.

The final month usually covers:

  • Final inspection. The City confirms the completed work meets code before the space is cleared for use.
  • Punch list. A walkthrough catches the small details - touch-up paint, adjustments, and last fixes - so nothing gets missed.
  • Closeout and move-in. Final cleaning, documentation, and handing the finished home back to you.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does a whole house remodel take month by month?

A whole house remodel typically spans 6 to 12 months. Roughly months 1 to 3 cover design and permitting, then demolition, structure, and rough systems fill the early construction months, followed by insulation and drywall, then finishes, with final inspection and move-in at the end.

What happens first in a remodel?

Planning happens first - not demolition. The opening months go to design, engineering, and permitting. Once permits are approved, the first physical step on site is demolition, which exposes the structure and systems the rest of the schedule depends on.

How long is the design and permit phase?

Design and permitting typically take about 1 to 3 months. Design and construction drawings run in parallel with plan check at the City of San Jose, and permitting is the variable that moves the schedule most, so we submit early to protect the timeline.

When do finishes go in?

Finishes go in during the later construction months, after insulation and drywall close the walls and rough inspections pass. Flooring, cabinets, tile, paint, and fixtures are the visible layer that goes on last, over finished walls.

What is the last step of a remodel?

The last step is the final inspection, followed by a punch list of touch-ups and closeout before you move back in. The final inspection confirms the work meets code, and the punch list catches the small details before sign-off.

Key Takeaways

  • A whole house remodel usually runs 6 to 12 months from first design meeting to move-in.
  • Months 1 to 3 go to design and permitting - no walls come down until permits are approved.
  • Construction moves from the structure outward: demolition, structure, rough systems, insulation and drywall, then finishes.
  • The final month is inspection, punch list, and move-in - a short phase where the project gets signed off and handed back.
