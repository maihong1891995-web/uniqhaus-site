Owner's Rep vs Design-Build: Do You Need a Construction Manager?

An owner's rep manages your architect and contractor for you. Design-build makes one team answer for both. Here is how to choose for a Bay Area remodel or custom home.

Owner's Rep vs Design-Build: Do You Need a Construction Manager?

An owner's rep is a paid advisor who manages a separate architect and general contractor for you, at a fee on top of both. Design-build puts design and construction under one contract and one team, so no extra manager is needed. A rep makes sense for large custom homes, owners who already hired an architect, or absentee owners. For most whole-house remodels in the $200,000 to $1.5 million range, design-build gives you a single point of accountability without the added layer.

If you are weighing an owner's rep vs design-build for a large remodel or custom home in San Jose or elsewhere in the Bay Area, the real question is how many parties you want between your budget and the finished house. An owner's representative is a paid advisor who sits on your side of the table and watches an architect and a general contractor you hired separately. Design-build puts design and construction under one contract, so one team answers for both. Both can work. They solve the same problem, which is keeping a $200,000 to $1.5 million project on plan, in different ways, and they cost you different amounts of time and money.

This guide is for homeowners who already know the basics of design-build and are now deciding whether to add a professional manager on top of a traditional team. If you need the basics first, read what a design-build firm actually does, or the side-by-side of design-build vs a general contractor. Here we focus on the owner's rep: what they do, what California law says about them, how they charge, and when one is worth it.

What does an owner's rep actually do on a home project?

An owner's rep (also called a construction consultant or homeowner project manager) manages the project on your behalf, but does not design it and does not build it. Think of them as your hired eyes and ears. On a residential job, their work usually falls into three phases.

Before construction

  • Helping you set a realistic budget and schedule before drawings start.
  • Helping you select and contract the architect, interior designer, engineers, and general contractor.
  • Reviewing drawings for gaps that tend to become change orders later, such as missing finish specs or unclear allowances.
  • Running or reviewing the bid process and leveling bids so you compare the same scope.

During construction

  • Attending site meetings and tracking progress against the schedule.
  • Reviewing pay applications and change order requests before you approve them.
  • Keeping a log of decisions, open questions, and who owes what to whom.

At closeout

  • Walking the punch list, collecting warranties and lien releases, and confirming final inspections are signed off.

What an owner's rep does not do matters just as much. They do not hold the design contract, so they cannot fix the drawings themselves. They do not hold the construction contract, so they cannot direct crews, guarantee a price, or warranty the work. When a problem shows up, they can document it, push on it, and advise you. The fix still has to come from the architect or the contractor, and you still sign the checks.

Does an owner's rep need a contractor license in California?

On a home improvement project, often yes. California law treats certain consultants as contractors who must hold a license from the Contractors State License Board (CSLB). Under Business and Professions Code section 7026.1, as amended by AB 2237 in 2012, a consultant on a home improvement contract counts as a contractor if they either:

  • provide or oversee a bid for the construction project, or
  • arrange and set up work schedules for contractors and subcontractors and maintain oversight of the construction project.

That describes much of what a full-service owner's rep sells. Purely advisory work, such as giving you a second opinion on a set of drawings or a single bid, may fall outside that definition, but the line depends on the facts. If a rep will run bids or manage trades on your remodel, ask for their CSLB license number and check it on the CSLB license lookup before you sign. Our guide on how to choose a contractor in the Bay Area walks through that check step by step.

One more trap: some homeowners pull an owner-builder permit and hire a consultant to "run it." The CSLB is clear that an owner-builder takes on the project's legal responsibilities, including supervising, scheduling, and paying subcontractors, and may be treated as an employer if anyone other than a licensed subcontractor works on the job (CSLB owner-builder responsibilities). A consultant does not take that responsibility off your shoulders.

How do owner's reps charge, and what does that add?

Owner's reps are paid on top of your architect and builder, so the fee is an added layer, not a replacement for any other role. Fees vary widely by firm and project size, and there is no official published rate for residential work, so ask every candidate for a written fee proposal. The common structures are:

  • Hourly. You pay for time used. Works for limited help, like a bid review. Hard to cap on a long job.
  • Fixed monthly retainer. A set amount for each month of the project. Easy to budget, but a slow project costs more months.
  • Percentage of construction cost. The fee rises with the budget. Ask how change orders affect it, since a percentage fee grows when scope grows.
  • Fixed project fee. One number for a defined scope of services. Ask what happens if the schedule runs long.

When you add it up, a traditional team with a rep has three sets of overhead: the design team's fee, the general contractor's overhead and profit, and the rep's fee. That can be a sound trade on a complex estate. On a typical whole-house remodel, it is worth asking whether you are paying a third party to manage a handoff that design-build removes in the first place.

How do the three delivery models compare?

There are three common ways to deliver a large residential project, and the owner's rep fits most naturally into the first. We cover the first and third in depth in design-build vs general contractor, so here is the short version with the rep's role in view.

  • Design-bid-build with an owner's rep. You hire an architect, the drawings go out to bid, you hire a GC, and a rep manages the whole chain for you.
  • Construction manager at-risk (CM at-risk). A builder joins during design to give cost and constructability input, then takes on the construction contract, often with a guaranteed maximum price. Common on commercial and public work, less common on single homes.
  • Design-build. One firm holds both design and construction under one contract.

The column that matters most is accountability. With separate contracts, a drawing gap becomes a debate about who pays: the architect says it was buildable, the contractor says it was not drawn. A good rep shortens that debate. Design-build removes the debate, because the people who drew it are the people who build it.

When does hiring an owner's rep make sense?

An owner's rep earns the fee in a few clear situations, and we would tell you so in a consultation. Consider one if:

  • You are building a large, complex custom home with many specialty consultants, such as a landscape architect, lighting designer, AV, and pool, and you want one professional coordinating all of them.
  • You have already hired an architect and plan to bid the drawings to several GCs. A rep can level those bids and police the contract for you.
  • You live out of the area or travel constantly and need someone on site every week who reports only to you.
  • You manage several properties or projects at once and want an experienced person running the paperwork.
  • Your project has a lender, partner, or family trust that needs independent progress reports before releasing funds.

An owner's rep is usually a weaker fit when the main goal is simply to avoid juggling an architect, a designer, and a contractor. Adding a fourth party to manage three others is one way to fix that. Hiring one team that does all three is another.

Where do budget overruns actually come from?

Most surprise costs on large remodels come from the gap between design and construction, not from the build itself. Plans get drawn without current pricing. Bids come back with different assumptions and vague allowances. Once walls open, unclear details turn into change orders. That is why so many homeowners ask about a construction consultant in the first place.

A rep attacks that gap from the outside. They review drawings, compare bids line by line, and push back on change orders. That helps, but they are still managing two contracts that were never designed to fit together.

Design-build attacks it from the inside. Because the same team prices the project while it designs it, cost feedback happens before drawings are final. At UniqHaus, pricing is itemized, and once you approve it, the price is fixed. The number you approve is the number you build. Changes you request after approval are priced as change orders, and we still recommend a 10 to 20 percent contingency for hidden conditions in older homes. You also get structural and cosmetic warranties from the same firm that drew the plans: 10 years on structural work (foundation and framing) and 2 years on cosmetic workmanship.

A common objection is that design-build must cost more. Often it costs less, because you are not paying for duplicated roles or an extra management layer, and you get real pricing early instead of after bids come back. For more on where overruns hide, see our breakdown of hidden costs in a whole-house remodel.

What does one accountable team look like in practice?

Our Hawthorne Ave project in Campbell is a good example of a job where many homeowners would consider an owner's rep. It was a whole-home remodel with an addition that took the house from about 1,700 to about 3,117 square feet, with 5 bedrooms and 5 bathrooms, an open floor plan, full electrical and plumbing replacement, and custom cabinetry. It was completed in 2025.

That scope touches architecture, structural work, interiors, permitting, and every major trade. Run as separate contracts, it would mean an architect, an interior designer, a permit expediter, and a GC, plus someone to keep them aligned. On Hawthorne Ave, one UniqHaus team handled the architecture, interiors, permit drawings and city coordination, and construction. The homeowners had one contract and one team to call.

That is the model behind our whole-home remodeling and custom home work. We have built in the Bay Area for more than 21 years and completed more than 162 projects under CSLB license #1158697.

How should you decide between the two?

Start with three honest questions about your project:

  1. Have you already signed with an architect? If yes and the drawings are well along, a rep may help you bid and build them. If no, design-build lets one team carry the project from first sketch.
  2. Who will be available to make decisions every week? If you will be away for long stretches, a rep who reports only to you can be worth the fee. If you can meet regularly, one design-build team usually gives you direct access to the people doing the work.
  3. Where do you want the cost risk to sit? A rep helps you manage risk spread across several contracts. Design-build puts design and construction under one contract and one price you approve.

Whichever way you go, verify every license, read the contract scope closely, and ask how changes are priced. If you want a second set of eyes on your plans, a design-build consultation is free and carries no obligation.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a construction manager for a home remodel?

Most homeowners do not, if one team is already responsible for both design and construction. A separate construction manager helps most when you hold several contracts yourself, such as an architect plus a bid-out general contractor, or when you cannot be available to make decisions each week.

Is a construction consultant for homeowners in the Bay Area required to be licensed?

On a home improvement project in California, a consultant who runs or oversees bids, or who schedules and oversees contractors, is treated as a contractor and needs a CSLB license under Business and Professions Code 7026.1. Always check the license number on the CSLB website before you hire.

What is the difference between an owner's rep and a general contractor?

A general contractor holds the construction contract, directs the crews, and is responsible for building the work. An owner's rep holds neither the design nor the construction contract. They advise you, review bids and invoices, and track progress, but they cannot guarantee the price or warranty the work.

Can I hire an owner's rep and a design-build firm together?

Yes. Some owners, especially lenders, trusts, or out-of-town clients, keep a rep to review progress and pay requests on a design-build job. On most single-family projects it is optional, because design-build already gives you one accountable team and itemized pricing.

How much does an owner's representative cost for a custom home?

There is no standard published rate for residential work. Reps typically charge hourly, a monthly retainer, a fixed project fee, or a percentage of construction cost. Ask for a written proposal and how change orders or a longer schedule affect the fee.

Is a homeowner project manager worth it on an owner-builder permit?

Be careful. Under an owner-builder permit you keep the legal responsibility for the project, including supervising and paying subcontractors, even if you hire a consultant. Hiring a licensed builder under a normal permit moves that responsibility to the contractor.

Key Takeaways

  • An owner's rep adds a third fee layer on top of the architect and the general contractor; design-build needs no separate manager.
  • In California, a consultant who runs bids or schedules trades on a home improvement project must hold a CSLB license (B&P Code 7026.1).
  • A rep makes the most sense for large custom homes, owners who already hired an architect, and absentee owners.
  • With UniqHaus design-build, the itemized price you approve is fixed, and we still advise a 10-20 percent contingency for hidden conditions.
