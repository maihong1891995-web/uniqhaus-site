Choosing a contractor in the Bay Area is the single decision that most determines whether your remodel, addition, or custom home goes smoothly - and it is also where the most expensive mistakes happen. The good news is that vetting is not guesswork: California gives you a clear set of things to verify, from an active license to a legally capped down payment, and each one filters out a category of risk. This is a practical checklist you can run before you sign anything. A related choice sits alongside it - whether to hire a general contractor or a single design-build team - which we cover in Design-Build vs General Contractor, and the specific interview to run is in Questions to Ask Before Hiring a Contractor.

How do you choose a contractor in the Bay Area?

You choose a Bay Area contractor by verifying four things before anything else: an active CSLB license, general liability insurance, workers' compensation, and bonded status - then checking references and getting the full scope in a written contract. In California, a contractor's license is legally required for any job of $500 or more in combined labor and materials, so on any real remodel an unlicensed bid is disqualifying, not a discount.

Two rules protect you beyond the paperwork. First, the contractor - not you - should pull the building permit, because whoever pulls it takes on responsibility as the builder of record. Second, California caps the down payment on a home-improvement contract at 10% of the price or $1,000, whichever is less, so a demand for a large deposit is a violation, not a negotiation. How to verify a California contractor license (CSLB)

Verifying a license takes about two minutes: look up the license number on the California State License Board (CSLB) website and confirm it is active, matches the business name on the bid, and carries the classification for your type of work. A license is required for any job of $500 or more in combined labor and materials, which covers essentially every remodel, addition, ADU, or custom home - so an unlicensed contractor on that scale is an immediate stop.

Do not accept a license number read aloud or a photo of a card; check it yourself on the CSLB record. While you are there, confirm the license is in good standing and that the bond and workers' comp status show as active - the same board record ties these together. If a contractor is reluctant to give you a number to verify, treat that reluctance as the answer. For larger builds where classification and scope matter most, our custom homes and architecture pages explain how the work is organized under one licensed team.

Insurance, bonding, and the down-payment rule

A license alone is not enough - you also want proof of general liability insurance, workers' compensation, and a bonded status, because these are what protect your money and property if something goes wrong. Liability insurance covers damage to your home during construction; workers' comp protects you from liability if a worker is injured on site; and the contractor bond gives you recourse if work is abandoned or defective. Ask for a current certificate of insurance and confirm coverage is active before any crew begins.

The clearest legal protection is the down-payment rule. Under California law, the down payment on a home-improvement contract cannot exceed 10% of the contract price or $1,000, whichever is LESS. The table below shows how that plays out at a few common Bay Area project sizes.

Contract price10% of priceLegal maximum down payment
$8,000$800$800 (10% is less than $1,000)
$25,000$2,500$1,000 ($1,000 is less than 10%)
$150,000$15,000$1,000 ($1,000 is less than 10%)

The pattern surprises most homeowners: on any project above $10,000, the legal cap is just $1,000, because $1,000 is always the smaller of the two figures. A contractor who asks for a third or half down, or who wants cash, is violating the rule - and the rest of the payments should follow a written schedule tied to completed milestones, never to a calendar. This is also why the contractor, not you, should pull the permit: it keeps them accountable as the builder of record, which our permitting service handles as part of a single contract.

Red flags that signal a bad contractor

Most bad outcomes are avoidable because the warning signs show up before you sign. If you see any of the following, stop and re-verify before committing a dollar:

  • No CSLB license - or a number they will not let you verify. On a $500+ job this is not a gray area; it is disqualifying.
  • A large or cash down payment. Anything over the 10%-or-$1,000 cap (whichever is less) breaks California law and is the classic pattern before a contractor disappears.
  • No written contract. A handshake and a verbal price leave scope, schedule, and payment terms undefined - and leave you with no recourse.
  • They want you to pull the permit. This shifts builder-of-record liability onto you and usually means they cannot or will not stand behind the work.
  • Pressure to decide today or a "discount" that expires overnight. Legitimate Bay Area contractors are booked out and do not need to rush you.
  • No proof of insurance or workers' comp. Without it, damage or an on-site injury can land on your homeowner's policy - or on you personally.

Any one of these is enough reason to walk. A trustworthy contractor treats the checklist as normal and hands over documentation without friction. If you want the flip side - the exact questions that surface these issues in a first meeting - our interview checklist pairs directly with this list.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you choose a contractor in the Bay Area?

Verify an active CSLB license, general liability insurance, workers' comp, and bonded status, then check references and get everything in a written contract. A California license is required for any job of $500 or more in combined labor and materials, and the contractor - not you - should pull the permits.

How do I verify a California contractor license?

Look up the license number on the California State License Board (CSLB) website to confirm it is active and matches the business name. A license is legally required for any job of $500 or more in combined labor and materials, so an unlicensed contractor on a real remodel is an immediate red flag.

How much can a contractor ask for as a down payment in California?

California law caps the down payment on a home-improvement contract at 10% of the contract price or $1,000, whichever is LESS. A contractor who asks for a large deposit up front - half down, or cash only - is violating that rule and is a serious warning sign.

Should the contractor or the homeowner pull the permit?

The contractor should pull the permits, not the homeowner. When an owner pulls the permit, they take on liability as the de facto builder. A licensed contractor filing at SJPermits.org keeps responsibility where it belongs and is a sign the work is being done by the book.

What are red flags when hiring a contractor?

The biggest red flags are no CSLB license, a request for a large or cash down payment, no written contract, pressure to decide today, and asking you to pull the permit yourself. Any one of these is reason to stop and re-verify before signing anything.

Is a design-build firm easier to vet than a general contractor?

A design-build studio carries one team and one contract for both design and construction, so accountability, licensing, insurance, and permitting all sit with a single firm. That removes the finger-pointing that happens when a separate designer and contractor blame each other.

Key Takeaways

  • Verify four things first - active CSLB license, general liability insurance, workers' comp, and bonded status - then references and a written contract; a license is required for any job $500 or more.
  • California caps the down payment at 10% of the contract price or $1,000, whichever is less - so on almost any real project the legal maximum is just $1,000.

  • No license, a large or cash deposit, no written contract, being asked to pull the permit yourself, or high-pressure tactics are all reasons to stop and re-verify.
