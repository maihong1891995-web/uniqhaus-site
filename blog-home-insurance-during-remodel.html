Homeowners insurance during a remodel still protects your house, but it was written for a home people live in, not a job site. For Bay Area homeowners planning a whole-house remodel in San Jose or the South Bay, the real risk is not a missing policy. It is a gap between policies: your homeowners coverage, your contractor's liability and workers' comp, and sometimes a builder's risk policy each cover a different piece.

This guide shows who typically covers what, when to call your insurer, and how to close the gaps before demolition starts. It is general information, not insurance or legal advice. Policy wording varies by carrier, so confirm every point with your own agent or broker.

Does your homeowners policy still cover the house while it is being remodeled?

Usually yes, for the existing structure, as long as you keep the policy active and tell your insurer what is happening. The California Department of Insurance tells homeowners to "discuss any modifications to your home, in writing, with your agent, broker, or insurer" in its residential insurance guide. A remodel changes the risk your carrier agreed to insure: more people on site, open walls, temporary power, stored materials, and sometimes an empty house.

What your policy is less likely to do is act as a construction policy. It was priced for a finished, occupied home. Three things tend to fall outside it or sit in a gray zone:

  • The new work itself before it is finished. Framing, windows waiting to be installed, and a pallet of tile in the driveway may or may not count as part of your dwelling until they are built in.
  • Injuries to the people doing the work. That exposure belongs to the contractor's workers' compensation, not your policy.
  • Losses while the house sits empty. The CDI guide lists "losses to house vacant for 60 days or more" among common exclusions.

None of this means you are uninsured. It means you need to know which policy answers which question before something happens, not after.

Who covers what during a remodel?

Each common remodel risk usually lands on one of four policies. The table below shows the typical pattern and the question to ask. "Typically" matters here: your contract, your carrier, and the scope of work can shift the answer.

Notice the pattern. Your policy protects the house you already own and your liability as the owner. The contractor's policies protect against their mistakes and their people. Builder's risk protects the project in progress. Gaps appear where those edges meet.

Do you need builder's risk insurance as a homeowner in California?

Sometimes, and mostly on large projects. The NAIC glossary of insurance terms describes builder's risk as coverage for buildings under construction, including materials and equipment used in the course of construction. The California Department of Insurance commercial insurance guide describes it as coverage for a building or structure under construction, often written for a one-year minimum term, with reporting or renovation forms that let coverage follow the stage of completion.

Whether you need one depends on the size of the job and how your contract assigns the risk. On a kitchen refresh, your existing homeowners policy plus the contractor's coverage is often enough. Builder's risk becomes a real conversation when:

  • The project is a major addition, a full gut, or a teardown and rebuild, where much of the value is new construction.
  • A large amount of material will be stored on site before installation.
  • Your homeowners carrier says it will limit or decline coverage during major construction.
  • Your lender or construction loan requires it.

Ask two questions early. First, does your contractor carry builder's risk for projects like yours, or expect you to? Second, does your homeowners carrier offer a renovation endorsement instead? Either answer can work. What does not work is both sides assuming the other one has it. Your contract should say in writing who insures the work in progress.

Do you need to tell your insurance company about a remodel?

Yes. Call before work starts, and confirm in writing. The CDI guide is direct about putting modifications in writing, and it also recommends keeping records of "updates, renovations, and improvements to your home." The call itself is usually short. Have these answers ready:

  1. Scope: what is being changed, and whether walls, roof, or structure will be opened.
  2. Dates: expected start and finish. A whole-house remodel often runs 6 to 12 months from design through construction, with 4 to 9 months of actual building.
  3. Occupancy: whether you will live in the house, and if not, for how long.
  4. Contractor: the company name and CSLB license number.
  5. Value: the contract amount, so your agent can talk about dwelling coverage now and after completion.

Then ask the carrier three things in return. Will coverage change during construction? Is there a vacancy or unoccupancy limit that applies to my plans? Should I raise my dwelling limit now, at completion, or both? Write down who you spoke with and the date. If the answer matters, ask for it by email.

What happens to your coverage if you move out during the remodel?

This is the gap that catches the most careful homeowners. Many families move out for a major remodel, and our guide on whether to move out during a remodel covers that decision. Once the house is empty, your policy's vacancy terms come into play. The CDI guide lists losses to a house vacant for 60 days or more as a typical exclusion, and a whole-house remodel can easily run longer than that.

Two details matter:

  • Definitions vary. Some policies treat "vacant" (no people and no furnishings) differently from "unoccupied" (furnishings remain, no one living there). Some carriers treat a house under active renovation differently from one that is simply empty. Only your policy wording answers this.
  • Temporary housing is your cost. Additional living expenses coverage pays for living elsewhere when a covered loss makes your home unlivable. The Insurance Information Institute explains in how much homeowners insurance you need that this coverage pays the cost of temporarily living elsewhere while your home is rebuilt. A planned move-out for a remodel is a choice, not a covered loss, so budget rent separately.

If you will be away, ask your agent whether a vacancy permit, renovation endorsement, or separate vacant-home policy fits. Plan regular site visits and make sure the contractor locks up and secures the site each day.

Why hiring unlicensed workers puts your own coverage at risk

Worker injuries are the biggest exposure a homeowner can create. The Contractors State License Board states on its guide to finding the right contractor that in California, a contractor with employees is required to carry workers' compensation insurance, and that if an injured worker's contractor has no insurance, "you could be financially liable to pay for injuries and rehabilitation." The same page warns that your homeowner's insurance "may or may not cover those costs."

The CSLB's owner-builder risks page makes the same point for owners who act as their own general contractor: if subcontractors are unlicensed or lack liability or workers' comp insurance and get hurt, you could be asked to pay through your homeowners policy or face lawsuits. A low cash bid from an unlicensed crew can turn into the most expensive line in your budget.

Three habits protect you:

  • Check the license and workers' comp status with the CSLB Instant License Check. The CSLB notes that insurance information is available on the contractor's license history page.
  • Ask for a certificate of insurance for general liability, and request that your name and address appear on it for this job.
  • Ask who will actually be on site, and confirm that subcontractors carry their own coverage.

The CSLB also notes that commercial general liability insurance is not legally required for every contractor, but it is what covers damage to your property. If a contractor does not carry it, they should be able to explain how losses would be paid. For the full vetting process, see how to choose a contractor in the Bay Area and the questions to ask a contractor before you sign.

Update your coverage when the remodel is finished

The last step is the one most people skip: raising your dwelling coverage once the work is done. Your dwelling limit should reflect what it would cost to rebuild your home, not its market price. The Insurance Information Institute notes that upgrades such as a renovated kitchen or added bathroom raise rebuilding cost, and the CDI guide warns that many California homes destroyed in past wildfires were underinsured. It advises reviewing your dwelling limit "initially and upon renewal."

A remodel can change the rebuild math a lot. Whole-house remodels in the Bay Area commonly run $100 to $500 per square foot. If you added square footage, replaced systems, or upgraded finishes, the old limit may no longer rebuild the home you now own. Send your agent the final contract value, the finished square footage, and a summary of upgrades. Keep your permits, final inspection sign-off, and receipts in one folder. They help both with setting coverage and with any future claim.

It is also a good time to revisit liability. The Insurance Information Institute notes that most homeowners policies start at $100,000 of liability coverage and that many advisers now suggest $300,000 to $500,000. A larger, more valuable home is worth a fresh look at that number.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does homeowners insurance cover damage caused by a contractor?

Usually the contractor's general liability insurance is the first place to look when their work damages your home. Your own policy may also respond depending on the cause, so report damage to both. Ask for a current certificate of insurance before work starts so you know the coverage exists.

Will my insurance premium go up after a remodel?

It can, because a larger or upgraded home costs more to rebuild, and your dwelling limit should rise to match. Your agent can tell you how your carrier prices the change.

Can my insurance company cancel my policy if I don't tell them about a renovation?

Policy terms vary, but an unreported change in risk can lead to problems at claim time. The California Department of Insurance advises discussing any modifications to your home in writing with your agent, broker, or insurer. Making the call before work starts is the simplest way to avoid that risk.

Who pays if a worker gets hurt on my property during a remodel?

The contractor's workers' compensation should pay if the contractor has employees and carries it, as California requires. If the contractor is uninsured, the CSLB warns you could be financially liable, and your homeowners policy may or may not cover it. Check workers' comp status on the CSLB license lookup.

Does homeowners insurance pay for a rental while my house is being remodeled?

Generally no. Additional living expenses coverage is for when a covered loss, like a fire, makes your home unlivable. Moving out for a planned remodel is a choice, so budget for temporary housing as part of the project.

How long can a house be empty before insurance stops covering it?

Many policies limit coverage after a set vacancy period, and the California Department of Insurance lists losses to a house vacant 60 days or more as a common exclusion. Some carriers use different definitions or day counts. Ask your agent for the exact wording and whether a vacancy endorsement is available.

Key Takeaways

  • Call your insurer before work starts and put the remodel details in writing, as the California Department of Insurance advises.
  • Watch the vacancy clause: a house vacant 60 days or more is a common exclusion, and many whole-house remodels run longer.
  • Verify workers' comp and liability on the CSLB license record; an uninsured crew can leave you liable for injuries.
  • Raise your dwelling limit after completion so it reflects rebuild cost, and revisit liability (many advisers suggest $300,000 to $500,000).
