Whole House Remodel Permits in Santa Clara County

Whole house remodel permits in Santa Clara County are issued by the city that your home sits in - San Jose, Sunnyvale, Santa Clara, and the other cities each run their own building department - while the county Building Division handles homes in unincorporated areas. Almost every part of a whole-home remodel needs a permit: structural, electrical, plumbing, mechanical, and additions. Plans go through plan check, a code review that usually takes several weeks to a few months, and a design-build firm prepares the package and manages that review for you.

Permits are the part of a whole house remodel that homeowners understand least and worry about most, and in Santa Clara County the first source of confusion is simple: which office is even in charge of your project. The county contains fifteen cities plus large unincorporated areas, and where your home sits decides who reviews your plans, issues your permit, and inspects the work. This guide explains how whole house remodel permits work in Santa Clara County - the city versus county question, what actually needs a permit, how the plan check process runs, what timelines to expect, and how a design-build team carries the whole package through review. It pairs with our whole house remodel permits in San Jose guide and our architecture and permitting service.

Who issues your permit: city or county

The single most important thing to establish first is jurisdiction. In Santa Clara County, the building department that issues your permit is decided by whether your home is inside an incorporated city or in an unincorporated area. If your property sits within a city, that city's building department has authority; if it sits in an unincorporated part of the county, the county's Building Division handles it.

Most homes in the county are inside a city. San Jose runs its own permit center, and so do Sunnyvale, Santa Clara, Cupertino, Mountain View, Palo Alto, Milpitas, Campbell, Los Gatos, Saratoga, and the rest. Each city has its own forms, its own submittal portal, its own fee schedule, and its own plan check queue, so a remodel in Sunnyvale follows Sunnyvale's process and one in Santa Clara follows Santa Clara's. There is no single countywide permit desk that covers every address.

Homes in unincorporated pockets - areas that belong to no city, including many hillside and rural parcels - are permitted by the County of Santa Clara Building Division instead. If you are not certain which category your address falls into, confirm it before you do anything else, because it determines every step that follows. A quick check with the jurisdiction, or a look at the county assessor and city boundary records, settles it. What needs a permit in a whole house remodel

On a whole-home project the honest answer is that most of the work needs a permit. A permit is generally required whenever you change a home's structure, alter its systems, or add space, and a gut remodel does all three. The main categories are consistent across jurisdictions in the county:

Type of workWhy a permit applies
StructuralRemoving or moving walls, changing the roof or foundation, and altering load paths require engineered plans and a permit
ElectricalNew circuits, rewiring, panel upgrades, and moved or added devices are permitted work under the electrical code
PlumbingRelocating fixtures, re-piping, and adding bathrooms or kitchen lines require a plumbing permit
MechanicalHVAC changes, new ductwork, and heating or ventilation equipment fall under mechanical permits
AdditionsAny increase in square footage, a new story, or converted space adds to the permit scope and the plan review

Purely cosmetic finish work - painting, replacing flooring like-for-like, swapping cabinet doors - generally does not require a permit. But those items are a small share of a true whole house remodel, and the moment a project moves a wall, opens up the electrical, re-routes plumbing, or adds a room, permits come into play. Many jurisdictions expect the trades on a whole-home job to be permitted together rather than piecemeal. When in doubt, treat the work as permitted and confirm with the building department; unpermitted structural or systems work is a problem that resurfaces at resale and at future inspections. The plan check and submittal process

Once you know your jurisdiction and scope, the permit itself comes through plan check: the building department's formal review of your drawings against the building code before it will issue a permit. The process follows a consistent shape whether you are in a city or the county.

First, you assemble and submit the package. For a whole house remodel that typically means architectural drawings, structural plans stamped by a licensed engineer where structural work is involved, and supporting documents such as energy compliance forms and, depending on scope, site and grading information. Most jurisdictions in the county now take submittals through an online portal.

Next, a plans examiner reviews the submittal. The examiner checks the drawings against the building, electrical, plumbing, and mechanical codes and any local requirements, and larger projects may be routed to more than one department - building, planning, and sometimes public works or fire. The department then returns a set of corrections, a written list of items to fix or clarify.

You revise the plans to answer every correction and resubmit. A whole-home project commonly goes through more than one review cycle before the plans clear, because the first submittal rarely resolves every code item. Once the corrections are satisfied and any planning or zoning conditions are met, the department approves the plans and issues the permit, and construction can begin under inspection. A complete, well-organized first submittal is the biggest lever you have on how many cycles this takes. See our building permit timeline guide for how the cycles stack up over a schedule.

Permit timelines: what to expect

Timelines are the question everyone asks, and the honest framing is that they vary by jurisdiction, project size, and how complete your submittal is. There is no single fixed processing time that applies to every city and every project, and any firm that promises an exact date before seeing your plans is guessing.

As a general expectation, a whole house remodel usually takes several weeks to a few months from submittal to permit issuance. That range reflects the reality that a whole-home project moves through multiple plan check cycles and often more than one department, so it takes longer than a simple single-trade permit. Smaller or cleaner submittals land at the shorter end; complex structural work, additions, or planning review push toward the longer end.

A few factors move the timeline in either direction. Completeness helps most: a package that answers the code up front draws fewer corrections and clears in fewer cycles. Turnaround on each correction cycle matters too, since a review clock effectively pauses while the plans sit on your side waiting to be revised. Department workload, staffing, and seasonal volume also shift the queue, and these change over time. Because of that, the reliable move is to contact the specific building department for its current review times rather than rely on a number you read once. We do not publish fixed processing times or fee figures here because each jurisdiction sets and updates its own, and treating an old number as a guarantee only creates false expectations.

How a design-build firm manages plan check

The permit process is where a design-build structure earns its keep. A design-build firm prepares the complete permit package and manages the plan check exchange with the building department, so you are not translating between a separate designer, engineer, and contractor while corrections pile up.

In practice that means one team produces the architectural and structural drawings and the supporting documents, submits them to the correct jurisdiction, and then owns the back-and-forth. When the plans examiner returns corrections, the people who drew the plans are the ones who answer them, revise the drawings, and resubmit - there is no gap where a designer blames the engineer or the contractor waits on both. Because design, permitting, and construction sit under one contract, the permit schedule stays connected to the construction schedule instead of living in a silo.

Frequently Asked Questions

Who issues whole house remodel permits in Santa Clara County?

The city where your home sits issues the permit if the property is inside an incorporated city such as San Jose, Sunnyvale, or Santa Clara, and the county Building Division issues it only when the home is in an unincorporated area. Check which jurisdiction your address falls under before you plan submittal, because each has its own building department, forms, and plan check queue.

What needs a permit in a whole house remodel?

Most of a whole house remodel needs a permit. Structural changes, electrical, plumbing, and mechanical work, and any addition to square footage all require permits, and many jurisdictions require them to be pulled together for a whole-home project. Cosmetic finish work like paint or flooring generally does not, but the moment you move a wall, alter systems, or add space, a permit applies.

What is the plan check process?

Plan check is the review a building department runs on your submitted drawings before it issues a permit. You submit stamped plans and supporting documents, a plans examiner checks them against the building code and any local requirements, and the department returns corrections. You revise and resubmit until the plans are approved, then the permit is issued. Larger whole-home projects usually go through more than one review cycle.

How long do whole house remodel permits take in Santa Clara County?

It varies by jurisdiction, project size, and how complete the submittal is. A whole house remodel usually takes several weeks to a few months from submittal to permit issuance because it goes through multiple plan check cycles and may involve more than one department. Contact the specific building department for its current review times, since queues and staffing change and a complete package moves faster than one that draws corrections.

How does a design-build firm manage the permit process?

A design-build firm prepares the full permit package, including architectural and structural drawings and the required supporting documents, and manages the plan check exchange with the building department. Because design, permitting, and construction sit under one team, corrections are answered by the people who drew the plans, resubmittals are handled directly, and the schedule from design through inspection stays coordinated.

Key Takeaways

  • Jurisdiction comes first: an incorporated city permits your remodel through its own building department, and the county Building Division handles unincorporated areas.
  • Most of a whole house remodel needs a permit - structural, electrical, plumbing, mechanical, and any addition - while purely cosmetic finish work usually does not.
  • Plan check is a review-and-correction cycle: submit stamped plans, answer corrections, resubmit until approved, then the permit issues.
  • Timelines run several weeks to a few months and depend on jurisdiction, scope, and submittal quality; confirm current review times with the specific department rather than trusting a fixed number.
