Seismic Retrofit During a Remodel: What Older Bay Area Homes Need

If your Bay Area home is wood-framed, built before 1980, and sits on a raised foundation, a seismic retrofit is worth adding to your remodel. A typical brace-and-bolt retrofit runs $3,000 to $7,000 per CRMP, and the state's Earthquake Brace + Bolt grant pays up to $3,000 for eligible homes. Doing it during a remodel shares the access, engineering, and permit you are already paying for.

A seismic retrofit during a remodel means bolting and bracing an older house while the floors, walls, and crawl space are already open, instead of hiring a separate crew years later. For Bay Area owners of pre-1980 homes on raised foundations, it is usually one of the most cost-effective structural upgrades you can add to a whole-house project in San Jose, Sunnyvale, or Campbell.

This guide explains how to tell whether your house is a candidate, what the work actually involves for each common home type, how the state's Earthquake Brace + Bolt grant fits in, and why the remodel is the right moment to do it. If you already know your project includes foundation repair or removing bearing walls, our structural and foundation remodel guide covers those costs. This article stays focused on the retrofit itself.

Does your Bay Area house need a seismic retrofit?

If your home is wood-framed, built before 1980, and sits on a raised foundation with a crawl space, it is a strong candidate. That is the exact profile the state targets: the California Residential Mitigation Program says an Earthquake Brace + Bolt retrofit "is only done on wood-framed, pre-1980 homes with a raised foundation" (CRMP, Earthquake Brace + Bolt).

That describes a large share of the South Bay. CRMP cites U.S. Census data showing that 63.8 percent of Santa Clara County housing units were built before 1980 (CRMP, San Jose retrofit guide). Many of the ranch homes and split-levels our clients own from the 1960s and 1970s were framed before modern anchoring and bracing details became routine.

The reason it matters is simple and not alarming. The USGS estimates a 72 percent probability of at least one magnitude 6.7 or greater earthquake in the San Francisco Bay region between 2014 and 2043 (USGS Fact Sheet 2016-3020). Nobody can predict when. What you can control is how well the house is attached to its foundation when it happens.

A quick self-check from the crawl space access:

  • Anchor bolts: Look at the wood sill sitting on the concrete. Do you see bolt heads with washers every few feet? Older homes often have few or none.
  • Cripple walls: These are the short stud walls between the foundation and the first floor. If you see bare studs with no plywood on them, they are unbraced.
  • Foundation type: A continuous concrete perimeter is what standard retrofits are written for. Brick, posts on piers, or a cracked or crumbling foundation needs an engineer first.

None of these findings means the house is unsafe today. They tell you what a retrofit would need to address.

What does a retrofit involve for your type of home?

The answer depends on how your house meets the ground. The national reference is FEMA P-1100, a prestandard developed for FEMA and the California Earthquake Authority that covers crawl space and cripple wall houses, living space over garages, hillside homes, and masonry chimneys. In California, a brace-and-bolt retrofit on a light wood-frame house is built to the California Existing Building Code, Chapter A3, which is the standard CRMP requires for grant-funded work (CRMP).

FEMA is careful about expectations, and so are we. The prestandard says its provisions are anticipated to improve earthquake performance, not to prevent all damage (FEMA P-1100). A retrofit lowers the odds that the house shifts off its foundation. It does not make a home "earthquake-proof," and anyone promising that is overselling.

Why is a remodel the best time to retrofit?

Because the hardest parts of a retrofit are getting access and getting approval, and a remodel already pays for both. Four things line up:

  1. Open framing. On a gut remodel, walls and sometimes subfloors come off. Crews can see the sill, the cripple walls, and the floor-to-wall connection directly instead of working on their backs through a tight crawl space access.
  2. One engineering package. If your remodel already needs a structural engineer for a new beam or an addition, the same engineer can design the retrofit. One set of calculations, one consistent load path from the roof to the foundation.
  3. One permit set. The retrofit sheets go into the same drawings that go through plan check, with the same inspector visiting at foundation and framing milestones.
  4. The right sequence. Structure goes in before plumbing, electrical, insulation, and finishes. Retrofitting after a remodel can mean cutting into new drywall or flooring you just paid for.

There is also a practical design benefit. When you remove interior walls to open a floor plan, some of the original bracing goes with them. The engineer has to replace that strength somewhere. Treating the crawl space retrofit and the new wall layout as one design is cleaner than solving them separately.

This is the same logic we use across older homes. If your house is from that era, our guide to updating a 1970s or 1980s Bay Area home covers the electrical, plumbing, and insulation items that tend to come up alongside structure.

How does the Earthquake Brace + Bolt grant work?

Earthquake Brace + Bolt (EBB) provides up to $3,000 toward a code-compliant retrofit of a qualifying house (CRMP). Income-eligible households can receive a supplemental grant of up to $7,000 more, which CRMP says may pay up to 100 percent of the retrofit cost while funds last. The income limit is published by the program and has changed between cycles, so check the current figure on the CRMP site before you count on it.

Here is what we have verified about how the program works:

  • House: wood-framed, built before 1980, raised foundation with a crawl space (CRMP).
  • Location: the house must be in an eligible ZIP code. The program expanded to more than 1,100 high-hazard ZIP codes statewide in 2025 (California Earthquake Authority). Look up your own ZIP on the CRMP site rather than assuming.
  • Occupancy: in 2025 the program opened to rentals and other non-owner-occupied homes for the first time (California Earthquake Authority).
  • Timing: registration opens for a limited time each year. In 2025 it opened in January and again on August 20, with the fall deadline extended to October 17. At the time of writing, a 2026 registration window has not been announced, and CRMP offers a sign-up to be notified when it opens.
  • Contractor: grant-funded work must be done by a contractor from the EBB directory (FEMA-trained, California-licensed general contractors) or by the owner acting as owner-builder (CRMP).

Two points matter if you are planning a remodel around the grant. First, register before you pull the retrofit permit. The program's rules expect the permit to be dated after your house is accepted, so a retrofit already permitted inside your remodel may not qualify. Second, the grant is written for the standard prescriptive retrofit. If your house needs an engineered solution, such as a soft story over a garage or a hillside foundation, the EBB grant does not cover that design.

Being honest about scale: on a whole-house remodel, $3,000 is helpful but small. The bigger saving is doing the work once, with access you already have.

What does a seismic retrofit cost?

CRMP puts a typical brace-and-bolt retrofit by a licensed contractor at $3,000 to $7,000, depending on location, house size, fees, and materials (CRMP). CRMP also notes that larger homes, hillside homes, homes with basements, and rooms over garages typically cost more (CRMP, San Jose guide).

That range is for a stand-alone, prescriptive retrofit on a house with a sound perimeter foundation. Three things can move the number in a remodel:

  • Foundation condition. Bolts need sound concrete to grip. If the foundation is cracked or undersized, repair comes first, and that is a separate, larger line item covered in our foundation guide.
  • Engineering. Taller cripple walls, garages, and slopes need a structural engineer. In a remodel, that engineer is often already on the project.
  • Shared costs. Mobilization, demolition, permit fees, and inspections are already in the remodel budget, so the retrofit adds mostly labor and hardware.

We price the retrofit as its own itemized line, so you can see exactly what it adds. Once you approve the scope, that price is fixed. We still recommend a 10 to 20 percent contingency on any older home, because crawl spaces do not always show everything until the work starts.

Do you need a permit for a seismic retrofit in San Jose?

Yes. The City of San Jose treats earthquake retrofits on single-family and duplex homes as permitted work (City of San Jose, Earthquake Retrofits). The city describes two paths. Under the EBB program, the contractor uses the state's standard plan set. Otherwise, you submit a retrofit plan with the dimensions, specifications, and details for foundation anchors and cripple wall bracing, and the city's standard path is written for cripple walls under 4 feet. Projects that do not fit those criteria go to plan review with a designed solution. Applications are filed online through SJPermits.org.

FEMA and the state developed those standard plan sets specifically to make home retrofits simpler and less expensive to permit (FEMA). Any new structure in your remodel, such as an addition or new bearing walls, is designed to the current California Residential Code adopted by the California Building Standards Commission.

Inside a remodel, the retrofit sheets simply join the rest of the drawing set. Our architecture and permitting team prepares the drawings and coordinates the engineer, and the general steps are in our San Jose building permit guide. Other cities in Santa Clara County have their own forms, but the same logic applies.

How we fold a retrofit into a whole-house remodel

We treat the retrofit as part of the structural scope from day one, not as an add-on found during demolition. That starts at the first walk-through, where we look in the crawl space alongside the kitchen and floor plan.

Our projects show the kind of access a full remodel creates. In Sunnyvale, the Blair remodel rebuilt every room from the studs, taking a 3 bed, 1 bath house of about 1,518 sq ft to a 4 bed, 3.5 bath home of about 1,918 sq ft in 2024. In Campbell, the Hawthorne Ave project grew a home from about 1,700 to about 3,117 sq ft with an addition, an open floor plan, and full electrical and plumbing replacement in 2025. These are not retrofit case studies, and we are not presenting them as such. They show what a house looks like when the framing is exposed and the structure can be addressed properly.

The sequence we follow:

  1. Assess. Crawl space, foundation, cripple wall height, garage and slope conditions.
  2. Design together. The retrofit, any new beams, and the new layout share one structural design.
  3. Price it clearly. The retrofit appears as an itemized line in a fixed price you approve.
  4. Permit once. One drawing set, one plan check, coordinated in-house.
  5. Build in order. Foundation and retrofit, then framing, then systems, then finishes.

Structural work we build, including foundation and framing, is covered by our 10-year structural warranty. You can read more about how we run older-home projects on our home remodeling service page.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it worth doing a seismic retrofit on a pre-1980 house in San Jose?

For a wood-framed house on a raised foundation, usually yes. CRMP puts a typical brace-and-bolt retrofit at $3,000 to $7,000, and the work targets the most common way older houses fail: sliding off the foundation. Doing it during a remodel lowers the cost further because access and permits are already in place.

How much does a cripple wall retrofit cost?

CRMP lists $3,000 to $7,000 for a typical brace-and-bolt retrofit by a licensed contractor, which covers anchor bolts and cripple wall bracing on a sound foundation. Taller cripple walls, hillside homes, basements, and rooms over garages typically cost more because they need engineering; for a typical house that needs more than the standard plan, budgets of roughly $5,000 to $20,000 are common, as covered in our foundation and structural guide.

Can I get the Earthquake Brace + Bolt grant if I am already remodeling?

Possibly. Your house must be pre-1980, wood-framed, on a raised foundation, and in an eligible ZIP code, and you must register when the program is open. The permit for the retrofit should be dated after your house is accepted, so register before the retrofit is permitted, and use a contractor from the EBB directory.

Does a house over a garage need a soft story retrofit?

Rooms over a garage with a wide door opening are a known weak point, and FEMA P-1100 includes a specific plan set for them. The fix is usually new braced panels or a steel frame at the garage front. It needs an engineered design and is not covered by the standard EBB grant.

Do slab-on-grade houses need seismic bolting?

Usually not in the brace-and-bolt sense, because there is no cripple wall or crawl space. When walls are opened during a remodel, the engineer can still check anchorage, and any new framing is built to the current California Residential Code.

Will a seismic retrofit make my house earthquake-proof?

No retrofit makes a house earthquake-proof. FEMA describes its retrofit provisions as improving earthquake performance, not preventing all damage. The goal is to keep the house on its foundation and reduce the chance of major structural repairs.

Key Takeaways

  • Wood-framed, pre-1980 homes on raised foundations are the main retrofit candidates; 63.8 percent of Santa Clara County housing was built before 1980.
  • A typical brace-and-bolt retrofit costs $3,000 to $7,000 per CRMP; garages, hillsides, and tall cripple walls need engineering and cost more.
  • Earthquake Brace + Bolt pays up to $3,000, plus up to $7,000 more for income-eligible households; register before the retrofit permit is issued.
  • San Jose requires a permit for earthquake retrofits; in a remodel it rides on the same drawing set, plan check, and inspections.
