Do You Need a Soils Report for a Custom Home or Addition?

Usually yes for a new custom home, and often for an addition. Under CRC R401.4 and CBC Section 1803, the building official can require a soils investigation where expansive or questionable soil is likely, and San Jose requires City Geologist clearance for development sites in state liquefaction zones. Order the report early in design, before the foundation is engineered, so findings do not force a redesign.

Do you need a soils report for an addition? Often yes, and for a new custom home in the Bay Area you should plan on one from the start. California's building codes let the local building official require a soils investigation whenever there is reason to expect expansive, compressible, or otherwise questionable soil, and much of the Santa Clara Valley floor sits on clay-rich alluvium that fits that description.

This guide is for homeowners planning a ground-up custom home or a sizable addition in San Jose and the South Bay. It covers when a report is required, what a geotechnical engineer actually measures, how those findings change your foundation and budget, and the point in design when ordering one saves the most money. It is general information. Your building department and a licensed geotechnical engineer make the call for your specific lot.

When is a soils report required for a new home or addition?

A soils report is required whenever your building official decides the site needs one, and in three common situations that decision is close to automatic. The rule itself is short. Under California Residential Code Section R401.4, when accepted soil data suggest expansive, compressible, shifting, or other questionable soils are likely, the building official determines whether to require a soil test, and the test must be done by an approved agency (see the 2025 CRC Section R401 text). If no full geotechnical evaluation is done, the code lets the engineer use conservative presumed soil bearing values from Table R401.4.1 instead.

The California Building Code, Section 1803, covers the same ground for engineered structures. It says that where a geotechnical investigation is required, a written report goes to the building official with the permit application, and Section 1803.5.3 directs the building official to require soil tests in areas likely to have expansive soil. One of the code's tests for "expansive" is an expansion index greater than 20 under ASTM D4829 (see the CBC Section 1803 text). Cities can also amend these sections locally, so the exact trigger varies by jurisdiction.

In practice, expect a soils report in these cases:

  • New custom homes. A ground-up house needs a new foundation designed for the whole structure. Structural engineers usually want site-specific data rather than presumed values, and many building departments ask for a report on new single-family construction.
  • Mapped hazard zones. If the lot is inside a state liquefaction or landslide zone, or a city geologic hazard zone, a site-specific study is typically part of getting a permit. More on San Jose's rules below.
  • Known problem soils. Expansive clay, uncompacted fill, a high water table, or a history of foundation movement on the existing house all give the building official reason to ask.

For a small single-story addition on a flat lot outside any hazard zone, some jurisdictions accept a foundation designed to presumed values, sometimes with deeper footings as a conservative trade-off. There is no single square-footage cutoff written into the state code, so the honest answer is to ask your building department early, before structural design starts.

What does a geotechnical report actually contain?

A residential geotechnical report combines field exploration, lab testing, and design recommendations your structural engineer uses directly. The engineer drills or digs at several locations around the planned footprint, logs each layer, pulls samples, and tests them in a lab. The finished report is part data and part instructions. Here is what each section tells you and why it matters to your design.

Finding in the reportWhat it meansHow it can change your design
Boring or test-pit logsThe layers of soil and fill under the footprint, and how deep each goesShows whether footings can bear on native soil or must go past loose fill
Allowable bearing capacityHow much load the soil can safely carry per square footSets footing width and depth; low values mean bigger or deeper footings
Expansion index and plasticityHow much the clay swells when wet and shrinks when dryMay call for deeper footings, a stiffened or post-tensioned slab, or piers below the zone of seasonal moisture change
Groundwater depthWhether water was encountered and at what levelAffects basements, crawlspace drainage, waterproofing, and excavation methods
Liquefaction and seismic analysisWhether loose, saturated sand could lose strength in a strong earthquake, plus the seismic site classCan lead to deep foundations, ground improvement, or a mat foundation; site class feeds the structural seismic design
Grading and compaction recommendationsHow to prepare the pad, including over-excavation and fill specsAdds earthwork scope and site testing during construction
Drainage and slab moisture recommendationsHow to keep water away from the foundation and out of the slabShapes grading, gutters, subdrains, and the vapor barrier under floors

The report usually ends with a list of construction observations the geotechnical engineer wants to make, such as inspecting footing excavations before concrete. Those site visits are part of the cost and part of the protection, because they confirm the soil in the trench matches the soil in the report.

What soil problems are common in San Jose and the South Bay?

The two issues that shape the most Bay Area foundations are expansive clay and liquefaction, and both are mapped or documented by public agencies. You can check your lot for each before you call an engineer.

Expansive clay on the valley floor

Clay soils swell in the wet season and shrink in the dry season. Over years, that cycle can lift and drop a foundation unevenly, which is behind many of the cracked slabs and sticking doors in older South Bay homes. A California Public Utilities Commission environmental review for a northeast San Jose project notes that Sunnyvale, Pescadero, and Tidal Marsh soil series on low-lying floodbasin deposits have high shrink-swell potential (CPUC, Geology and Soils section). To look up the mapped soil series on your own parcel, the USDA's free Web Soil Survey reports shrink-swell ratings by map unit. These maps are a screening tool, not a substitute for borings on your lot.

Liquefaction zones

Liquefaction happens when loose, water-saturated sand temporarily acts like a liquid during strong shaking. The California Geological Survey maps state Seismic Hazard Zones for liquefaction and earthquake-induced landslides under the Seismic Hazards Mapping Act (CGS Seismic Hazards Program). You can type an address into the state's EQ Zapp tool to see whether a property falls in a mapped zone. The USGS has also published liquefaction hazard maps for San Jose, Campbell, and other northern Santa Clara County cities under three earthquake scenarios.

San Jose geologic review

San Jose adds a city layer on top of the state maps. According to the city's Geological Hazard Review page, development sites inside a state liquefaction zone need a Geologic Clearance approved by the City Geologist before a grading or building permit is issued. Sites in the city's own Geologic Hazard Zone, defined in San Jose Municipal Code Chapter 17.10, need a Geologic Hazard Clearance, and geotechnical studies and mitigation may be required. Whether a given single-family addition triggers this review depends on the scope and the city's determination, so confirm it for your parcel through SJPermits.org or the Permit Center. Hillside lots in the foothills bring their own landslide and grading review, which is a separate topic from the valley-floor issues covered here.

How does a soils report change foundation design and budget?

A soils report rarely adds a large cost by itself, but its findings can move the foundation budget more than any other early decision. The report is the input. The foundation design is where the money shows up.

On a lot with firm, low-expansion soil, a conventional spread footing or slab often works with modest depth. On expansive clay, the same house might need deeper footings, a post-tensioned slab, or drilled piers with grade beams. In a liquefaction zone, the engineer may recommend a mat foundation, ground improvement, or deep foundations. Each step up adds concrete, steel, excavation, inspection, and schedule time.

The report's own fee depends on a few drivers rather than a single price:

  • Number and depth of borings. A larger footprint or a deeper investigation in a liquefaction zone means more drilling.
  • Site access. A tight backyard behind an existing house may need a smaller rig or hand equipment.
  • Lab testing scope. Expansion, consolidation, and liquefaction analysis each add testing time.
  • Hazard zone reporting. Reports that need City Geologist review usually involve more analysis and a review fee.
  • Construction observation. Footing inspections and compaction testing during the build are often billed separately.

For context, a Bay Area custom home generally runs $400 to $700+ per square foot, and a home addition generally runs $100,000 to $500,000+. Foundation design sits inside those numbers, which is why we recommend carrying a 10 to 20 percent contingency until the soils data and structural design are in hand. When should you order a soils report?

Order the soils report early in design, after you have a site survey and a rough footprint but before the structural engineer finalizes the foundation. Ordering too late is the most common and most avoidable mistake. If the report arrives after drawings are nearly done and calls for piers instead of a slab, the structural set, and sometimes the floor heights and drainage plan, have to be redrawn.

A practical sequence looks like this:

  1. Screen the lot. Check EQ Zapp, the soil survey, and your city's hazard maps. Note any past foundation repairs on the existing house.
  2. Get a boundary and topographic survey. The geotechnical engineer needs to know where the building will sit.
  3. Set a preliminary footprint. Borings should land inside or near the planned foundation, not in a random corner of the yard.
  4. Commission the geotechnical investigation. Fieldwork is usually short. Lab testing and the written report take longer.
  5. Design the foundation from the report. The structural engineer follows its bearing values and recommendations.
  6. Submit for permit with the report attached. The building department, and the City Geologist where applicable, reviews it alongside the plans.
  7. Schedule observations during construction. The geotechnical engineer confirms footing excavations and compaction before concrete is placed.

For an addition, one extra question matters: how will the new foundation meet the old one? If the existing house sits on shallow footings over expansive clay and the addition goes on deeper piers, the two parts can move differently over time. A good soils report and structural design plan for that joint from day one. Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a geotechnical report for a small home addition?

It depends on your building department and your lot. For a small single-story addition on a flat site outside any hazard zone, some jurisdictions accept a foundation designed to presumed soil values. If the lot has expansive clay, fill, or sits in a mapped liquefaction or geologic hazard zone, expect the building official to ask for one.

How do I know if my house is in a liquefaction zone?

Enter your address in the California Geological Survey's EQ Zapp tool, which shows state liquefaction, landslide, and fault zones. Your Natural Hazard Disclosure from when you bought the home may also list it. In San Jose, sites in a state liquefaction zone need City Geologist clearance before a building permit.

What does expansive soil mean for a foundation in San Jose?

Expansive clay swells when wet and shrinks when dry, which can move a shallow foundation unevenly over time. A soils report measures the expansion index, and the structural engineer responds with deeper footings, a stiffened or post-tensioned slab, or piers. Drainage that keeps water away from the foundation matters just as much.

How long is a soils report good for?

There is no single statewide expiration. Building departments and engineers may ask for an update letter if the report is several years old, the site has changed, or the design has moved. If you bought a lot with an existing report, ask the original engineer whether it still fits your planned footprint.

Can I use my neighbor's soils report?

Usually not as-is. The CBC lets the building official waive an investigation when satisfactory data from adjacent areas exists, but that is the official's decision, and soil can change over short distances. A nearby report is a good screening tool and may help your engineer scope fewer borings.

Who orders the soils report, the owner or the contractor?

The owner typically contracts with or pays for the geotechnical engineer, but the design team usually scopes it and coordinates the borings with the planned footprint. In a design-build setup like UniqHaus, we coordinate the report so its findings go straight into the structural design and your itemized price.

Key Takeaways

  • Under CRC R401.4 and CBC 1803, the building official decides when a soils investigation is required, often where expansive or questionable soil is likely.
  • The CBC treats soil with an expansion index above 20 (ASTM D4829) as one sign of expansive soil.
  • San Jose requires City Geologist clearance before a grading or building permit for development sites in state liquefaction zones.
  • Order the report after the survey and preliminary footprint, before foundation design, and carry a 10 to 20 percent contingency until results are in.
