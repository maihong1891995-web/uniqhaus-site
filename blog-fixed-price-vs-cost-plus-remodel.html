Fixed Price vs Cost-Plus: Which Remodel Contract Protects Your Budget?

The pricing model in your contract decides who pays when a remodel costs more than planned. Here is how fixed-price, cost-plus, and GMP contracts really move risk on a Bay Area remodel.

Fixed Price vs Cost-Plus: Which Remodel Contract Protects Your Budget?

For a fully designed remodel, a fixed-price contract usually protects your budget best, because the builder absorbs overruns on the agreed scope. A cost-plus contract gives full visibility into costs but leaves overrun risk with you and a percentage fee grows as costs grow. Either way, hold a 10 to 20 percent contingency for changes you request and conditions hidden behind walls.

When you compare remodel bids in San Jose or anywhere in the Bay Area, the pricing model matters as much as the price. A fixed-price contract puts most cost risk on the builder, while a cost-plus contract passes the real cost of labor and materials to you, plus a fee. On a $200,000 to $1 million whole-house remodel, that choice decides who pays when the work takes longer or costs more than planned. This guide shows how each model moves risk, what a fixed price can fairly leave out, and how to read a cost-plus fee before you sign.

How do fixed-price and cost-plus contracts work?

A fixed-price contract sets one total for a defined scope, and the builder absorbs the cost if their own labor or materials run over. A cost-plus contract has you pay the actual cost of the work, then a fee on top for the builder's overhead and profit. Most remodel contracts fall into one of four models:

  • Fixed price (lump sum). One number for the drawings and specs. If framing takes three extra weeks because the crew was slow, that is the builder's problem, not yours.
  • Cost-plus with a percentage fee. You pay every invoice, plus a fee of a set percentage of those costs. When costs rise, the fee rises with them.
  • Cost-plus with a fixed fee. You still pay actual costs, but the builder's fee is a set dollar amount. Overruns cost you more, but they do not raise the builder's profit.
  • Time and materials (T&M). A simpler form of cost-plus: hourly labor rates plus materials with a markup. Common for small repairs and emergency work, and rarely a good fit for a whole-house remodel.

A fifth option sits between them: cost-plus with a guaranteed maximum price (GMP). You pay actual cost plus the fee, but only up to a cap. Above the cap, the builder pays, except for changes you ask for. The table below shows how the models compare on what matters to you.

The pattern is simple. The more defined your scope is, the more a fixed price protects you. The less defined it is, the more any builder has to pad a fixed number, and the more cost-plus starts to make sense.

What does a fixed price not cover?

A fair fixed price covers everything shown in the drawings and specs, and it still leaves out three things: changes you request, conditions nobody could see, and allowances. Knowing these three is how you tell an honest fixed bid from one built to grow later.

1. Changes you ask for

If you move a wall, add a skylight, or upgrade the windows after the price is set, that is new scope, and it gets priced as a change order. Our hidden costs guide explains change orders in detail. The point here is that a fixed price fixes the scope you approved, not every idea you have later.

2. Unforeseen conditions

Older Bay Area homes from the 1950s to 1980s often hide problems behind plaster and under floors: old wiring, rot at the sill plate, galvanized pipe, or framing a past owner cut. A good contract names what counts as "unforeseen," says how it will be priced (for example, at the same unit rates as the bid), and requires your signed approval before work starts. Watch for a vague clause that lets almost anything count as hidden. If a builder never opened a wall or crawled the foundation before bidding, more of the risk will come back to you.

3. Allowances

An allowance is a set dollar amount for an item you have not chosen yet, such as tile, plumbing fixtures, or lighting. If the bid carries a $15,000 tile allowance and your tile costs $19,000, you pay the $4,000 difference. If it costs $12,000, you should get $3,000 back. Allowances are where a "fixed" price leaks most often, because a low allowance makes a bid look cheaper than it is. Ask three questions:

  • Is each allowance based on a real product at a real finish level, or a round guess?
  • Does the allowance cover material only, or material and installation?
  • Is the builder's markup applied to overages, and are savings credited back to you?

The fewer allowances a bid carries, the more fixed it really is. That is why choosing finishes during design, before pricing, matters so much. Our guide to what is included in a whole house remodel covers the scope items to confirm line by line.

How do you read a cost-plus fee?

A cost-plus fee is only as clear as the contract's definition of "cost," so the fee percentage alone tells you little. Two bids with the same fee can end up far apart. Before you compare them, check these points in the contract:

  • Percentage or fixed fee. A percentage fee grows with every overrun. A fixed fee does not, so the builder gains nothing from higher costs.
  • What counts as cost. Is the project manager's time billed as cost, or is it part of the fee? What about the site supervisor, dumpsters, portable toilets, permits, and small tools? These "general conditions" can add a lot if they sit outside the fee.
  • Markup on subcontractors. Some contracts add a markup to each subcontractor invoice and then charge the fee on top. That is a fee on a fee.
  • Audit rights. You should be able to see actual invoices, receipts, and timesheets, not just a monthly summary.
  • Discounts and rebates. If the builder gets trade pricing or a volume rebate, does that savings pass to you?
  • A cap. A guaranteed maximum price turns open-ended risk into a ceiling. Some GMP contracts split any savings under the cap between owner and builder, which rewards both sides for coming in low.

If a cost-plus bid cannot answer these clearly in writing, you do not have a budget. You have an estimate with no limit.

What does each model look like on a $400,000 remodel?

On the same $400,000 remodel, a fixed-price contract and a cost-plus contract can end tens of thousands of dollars apart once real work starts. The numbers below are a simplified illustration, not a client project or a quote. Assume a whole-house remodel where the builder expects $340,000 in direct cost for labor, materials, and subcontractors, and a fee or margin of about $60,000.

During construction, three things happen:

  1. Opening the kitchen wall reveals an old section of wiring that must be replaced: $12,000 of truly unforeseen work.
  2. You choose tile that runs $6,000 over the allowance.
  3. The crew loses time to slow work and rework, adding $30,000 in labor and materials that were inside the original scope.

In this illustration, the $18,000 of unforeseen work and tile overage reaches you under every model. That part is normal and is exactly what a contingency is for. The difference is the $30,000 the builder caused. Under a fixed price, it is not your cost. Under a percentage fee, you pay it and the builder earns more on it. In the GMP case, you pay part of it until costs hit the adjusted $438,000 cap. That is the core trade: cost-plus gives you visibility into every dollar, while a fixed price gives you protection from the builder's own mistakes.

Notice too that a 15 percent contingency on this job is $60,000. The fixed-price owner used $18,000 of it. The percentage-fee owner used about $56,470 of it, and most of that paid for the builder's own overrun.

When can cost-plus make sense?

Cost-plus is a reasonable choice when the scope cannot be defined well enough to price, and you trust the builder enough to give up a fixed number. That usually means one of these:

  • Emergency or damage repair. After a leak, fire, or failed structure, work has to start before anyone knows the full extent.
  • Very early, undefined scope. You want to start before design is finished, and you accept less budget certainty for speed.
  • Exploratory work in very old homes. Some older homes carry so much unknown that any fixed bid would be mostly padding.
  • Owners who want to decide as they go and are prepared to review invoices every month.

For most whole-house remodels, there is a better path than starting on cost-plus: finish the design first. When drawings, finishes, and site checks are done before pricing, the unknowns that make cost-plus attractive mostly disappear, and a fixed price no longer needs heavy padding. If you do choose cost-plus, ask for a fixed fee, open books, and a GMP once design is complete.

What California rules apply to either contract?

California's home improvement contract rules apply whether your contract is fixed-price or cost-plus. The Contractors State License Board (CSLB) summarizes three that affect your budget directly, drawn from Business and Professions Code section 7159 and section 7159.5:

  • Down payment cap. The down payment cannot be more than $1,000 or 10 percent of the contract price, whichever is less, according to the CSLB guide to home improvement contracts. Our contractor guide shows how that works at common project sizes.
  • Payments cannot get ahead of the work. The contract needs a detailed written payment schedule, and payments cannot exceed the value of work performed, apart from the down payment. On a cost-plus job, tie each payment to work in place and backed by invoices, not to calendar dates.
  • Changes must be in writing. If price or scope changes, the CSLB says it must be done with a written change order signed by you and the contractor before the change. The CSLB consumer guide (PDF) also advises turning any verbal promise into a written change order.

These rules protect you under both models, but they work best with a clear scope. A written change order is easy to judge when the original scope says exactly what was included.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a fixed-price or cost-plus contract better for a home remodel?

For most whole-house remodels with finished drawings and selected finishes, a fixed-price contract is better for budget certainty, because the builder absorbs overruns on the agreed scope. Cost-plus fits emergency work or projects that must start before the scope is defined. If you choose cost-plus, ask for a fixed fee, open books, and a guaranteed maximum price.

What is a typical cost-plus fee for a remodel?

There is no standard fee; it varies by builder and project, and the percentage alone is misleading. Check what the contract counts as cost, whether supervision and general conditions are billed separately, and whether subcontractor invoices carry an extra markup before the fee is added.

Can a fixed-price contract still go over budget?

Yes, but only for defined reasons: changes you request after approval, unforeseen conditions such as damaged framing or old wiring behind walls, and allowance items that cost more than the set amount. A contingency of 10 to 20 percent covers these. Overruns caused by the builder's own labor or materials on the agreed scope should not reach you.

What is a guaranteed maximum price contract?

A guaranteed maximum price (GMP) contract is cost-plus with a ceiling. You pay actual cost plus the fee up to the cap, and the builder pays costs above it, except for changes you request. Some GMP contracts share any savings under the cap between owner and builder.

How much can a contractor ask for upfront in California?

On a home improvement contract, California limits the down payment to $1,000 or 10 percent of the contract price, whichever is less. After that, payments cannot exceed the value of work performed, according to the CSLB. This applies to both fixed-price and cost-plus contracts.

Is time and materials a good way to pay for a remodel?

Time and materials works for small repairs and emergency work, but it is rarely a good fit for a whole-house remodel. You pay hourly labor and marked-up materials with no ceiling, so every delay adds to your cost. For a large project, define the scope through design first and price it as a fixed number.

Key Takeaways

  • A fixed price moves overrun risk on the agreed scope to the builder; cost-plus keeps it with you.
  • A fair fixed price still excludes three things: changes you request, truly unforeseen conditions, and allowance overages.
  • On cost-plus, prefer a fixed fee over a percentage, and ask for open books and a guaranteed maximum price.
  • In California, the down payment is capped at $1,000 or 10 percent, whichever is less, and every change needs a signed written change order.
