Tree in the Way? San Jose Tree Removal Permits for Additions and ADUs

On a single-family lot in San Jose, you need a tree removal permit for any tree with a trunk 38 inches or more in circumference at 4.5 feet above the ground, live or dead. Street trees go through the Department of Transportation, and heritage trees always need a permit. If you are building an addition or ADU, removal can be approved as part of your development permit, usually with a 1:1 replacement tree. See the city's Tree Removal Permits page.

If a tree stands where your addition, ADU, or rebuild needs to go, the tree removal permit in San Jose is often the first real blocker in planning. On a single-family lot, the city requires a permit for any tree with a trunk of 38 inches or more in circumference, measured 4.5 feet above the ground, and that applies even if the tree is dead. Street trees and heritage trees follow their own rules, handled partly by a different department.

This guide is not a general permit walkthrough. For how building permits move through SJPermits.org, see our San Jose building permit guide. Here we focus on one question: what do you do when a tree and your project want the same ground? You will find a decision table, what the city asks for, how tree removal fits into your development permit, and practical ways to design around a tree you would rather keep.

What counts as an ordinance-size tree in San Jose?

A single-trunk tree is ordinance-size in San Jose when its trunk measures 38 inches or more in circumference at 4.5 feet above the ground. That is roughly 12 inches in diameter, so many mature backyard trees qualify sooner than owners expect. The city's Tree Removal Permits page sets out the threshold, and the definition comes from Municipal Code Chapter 13.32, Tree Removal Controls.

  • Multi-trunk trees: add the circumference of each trunk at 4.5 feet. If the total is 38 inches or more, the tree is ordinance-size.
  • Live or dead: the code's definition of a tree covers both. A dead ordinance-size tree on a single-family or duplex lot still needs a permit.
  • Multifamily, commercial, and industrial lots: the city requires a permit to remove a tree of any size.

How to measure: wrap a soft tape around the trunk at 4.5 feet (54 inches) above natural grade. If you are close to 38 inches, treat it as ordinance-size until someone qualified confirms otherwise. Some older websites still quote a 56-inch threshold measured at 2 feet. That figure is out of date, so rely on the city's current page.

Most San Jose homeowners fall into one of six situations, and only one of them is usually permit-free. Use this table as a starting point, then confirm with the city before anyone cuts.

Two points catch people out. First, "it's dead" is not a shortcut on a single-family lot. Second, the strip of land between the sidewalk and the street usually belongs to the public right-of-way, so a tree there is a street tree even though you water it.

Street trees and heritage trees: a different path

Street trees are handled by the city's Department of Transportation, not the Planning Division. The DOT tree permits page states that property owners must get a permit before pruning, removing, or planting a street tree, and the city describes pruning and removal permits for street trees as no-cost. Removing a street tree without a permit can bring significant fines, so do not let a tree crew or a demolition sub decide this on site.

Heritage trees are a short list of trees the City Council has designated for special community significance. The list is adopted by Council resolution and covers more than 100 trees. You can check a specific tree on the city's Heritage Trees page, which links to a heritage tree map and the City Arborist. If your tree is on that list, assume removal will be difficult and plan around it from day one.

Why this matters for construction: a new driveway, a widened curb cut, or a utility trench for an ADU can run straight through a street tree's root zone. Even if you are not removing it, work near it may need DOT coordination. Identify these trees on your survey before design starts.

How tree removal ties into your addition or ADU permit

You can request tree removal as part of a development permit instead of filing a separate tree permit. Section 13.32.080 of the Tree Removal Controls chapter lets a Title 20 development permit serve as the tree removal permit, as long as the chapter's requirements are met. When a project only needs a building permit, a standalone tree removal permit through Planning is the usual route. Either way, the tree decision should be settled before your building plans are final, because it shapes the footprint.

The city does not approve removals automatically. Under the code, the decision-maker must find at least one of three things:

  1. Removal would not significantly frustrate the purpose of the ordinance, given the tree's size, type, condition, and location.
  2. The tree's location relative to a proposed improvement unreasonably restricts the economic development of the parcel.
  3. Public health or safety requires removal, for example disease, danger of falling, proximity to structures, or interference with utilities.

For a homeowner, the second finding is the one that usually connects to an addition or ADU. That is why your application should show the proposed project, why the tree conflicts with it, and what alternatives you considered. A clean site plan does more work here than a long letter.

Arborist reports. The city may require a certified arborist report for any removal when it needs more information, and dead-tree applications rely on one when photos are not enough. On a construction project, an arborist report also helps you decide which trees to keep and how to protect them.

Replacement trees. Approved removals come with replacement planting. The code requires suitable replacement trees on-site, or elsewhere in San Jose if on-site planting is not feasible, and the city's current application guidance describes a 1:1 ratio with a minimum 15-gallon tree for single-family and duplex lots. If you cannot replant on your lot, the city offers an in-lieu fee; the amount is set in the Planning fee schedule, so check the current figure rather than relying on older numbers you find online. Removing a tree without a permit first can mean higher replacement requirements later.

State ADU law. California's ADU rules limit how cities can restrict qualifying ADUs. Do not read that as permission to skip a tree permit. Tree removal is reviewed under its own ordinance, and the safe assumption is that a protected tree still needs city approval even when the ADU itself must be allowed. If you think a tree rule is blocking an ADU the state says you can build, raise it with Planning in writing and keep the response. For the ADU permit itself, see our ADU permit guide for Santa Clara County.

Can you design around the tree instead?

Often, yes, and it is usually cheaper in time than a contested removal. A tree you keep also adds shade and character that a new 15-gallon replacement will take years to match. These are the levers we look at first:

Site layout options

  • Shift or rotate the footprint. Moving an ADU a few feet, or turning it, can clear the root zone while still meeting setbacks.
  • Build up instead of out. A second-story addition or an ADU above a garage uses less ground near the tree.
  • Change the shape. An L-shaped addition can wrap a tree rather than covering it.
  • Move the hardscape. Driveways, patios, and paths are easier to relocate than rooms, and paving over roots is a common cause of tree decline.

Foundation choices near roots

A continuous perimeter footing needs a long, deep trench, which can sever major roots. Near a tree, engineers sometimes use a pier and grade beam foundation, where drilled piers carry the load and a beam bridges between them, so excavation happens at points instead of along a full line. Whether that works on your lot depends on soils, structural design, and the arborist's root findings. Treat it as an option to discuss, not a default. Our foundation guide covers how these decisions are made.

Utility routing and timing

  • Route new sewer, water, and electrical lines for an ADU away from the root zone, or ask whether boring under roots is an option.
  • Settle the tree question before construction drawings are finalized. Changing a footprint after plan check costs more than changing it in schematic design.
  • If removal is likely, file early, because the tree permit can become the long pole in your schedule.

Tree protection during construction

If you keep a protected tree, expect protection measures on your plans and on site. In San Jose these are typically written into the project arborist's report and the approved drawings, and they commonly include:

  • Protective fencing set up before demolition or grading starts, placed to keep equipment out of the root zone.
  • No storage, parking, or washout inside the fenced area, including concrete and paint cleanup.
  • Hand digging or arborist supervision where excavation must come close to roots, with clean cuts on any roots that must be cut.
  • Grade control: no added fill or cuts around the trunk that change drainage or bury roots.

The exact fence type, distance, and inspection points vary by project and are set by the arborist and the approved plans, so follow those documents rather than a generic checklist. In practice, most damage to retained trees comes from trucks, stockpiled material, and trenching late in the job, not from the original design. Assign one person to own tree protection for the whole schedule.

What this looks like on a real San Jose lot

Lot planning is where tree questions get answered. On our Bird Avenue project in San Jose, we took a roughly 1,046 sq ft home to about 2,207 sq ft, with 4 bedrooms and 3 baths, a new front porch, a rear patio, and an attached garage, all on a 7,405 sq ft lot. Fitting that much program onto a lot that size means placing every element, from the garage to the patio, with setbacks, access, and existing site features in mind before the first drawing goes to the city. The same discipline applies when a tree is one of those features.

Because UniqHaus handles architecture and permit drawings in-house alongside construction, the tree survey, arborist input, footprint, and foundation design stay in one plan. For ADUs specifically, see our ADU design and build service, and for additions, our home addition permit guide. We cannot promise the city will approve a removal. What we can do is give you a design that works with either outcome, so the tree decision does not stall the whole project.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a permit to cut down a tree in my backyard in San Jose?

Only if it is ordinance-size, a heritage tree, or a street tree. On a single-family or duplex lot, ordinance-size means a trunk 38 inches or more in circumference at 4.5 feet above the ground, measured as a total for multi-trunk trees.

Do I need a permit to remove a dead tree in San Jose?

Yes, if it is ordinance-size. The city requires a permit even for dead trees on single-family and duplex lots, and you may need a certified arborist report if photos cannot show the tree is dead or dying.

Can I remove a tree to build an ADU in San Jose?

Possibly, but you still need city approval for a protected tree. The removal can be reviewed alongside your development permit, and the city must make a finding such as the tree unreasonably restricting development of the lot. Shifting the ADU footprint is often faster than a contested removal.

How many replacement trees does San Jose require?

The city's current guidance for single-family and duplex lots describes a 1:1 ratio with a minimum 15-gallon tree. If you cannot plant on-site, you can pay an in-lieu fee set in the Planning fee schedule. Confirm current requirements on the Tree Removal Permits page.

Who owns the tree between the sidewalk and the street in San Jose?

That strip is usually public right-of-way, so the tree is treated as a street tree. You need a permit from the Department of Transportation before pruning or removing it. Start with the DOT tree permits page or the City Arborist.

How do I find out if my tree is a heritage tree in San Jose?

Check the city's Heritage Trees page, which links to the heritage tree map, or ask the City Arborist. The list is adopted by City Council resolution and includes more than 100 trees.

Key Takeaways

  • Ordinance-size in San Jose means a trunk of 38 inches or more in circumference at 4.5 feet, about 12 inches in diameter.
  • Dead ordinance-size trees on single-family lots still need a permit; street trees go through DOT, not Planning.
  • Removal can be approved within a development permit, but the city must make 1 of 3 findings under Chapter 13.32.
  • Expect 1:1 replacement with a minimum 15-gallon tree on single-family lots, or an in-lieu fee if you cannot replant.
